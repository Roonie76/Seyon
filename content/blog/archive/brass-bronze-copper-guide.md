Three metals, sold interchangeably, that behave completely differently in a kitchen. Buying the wrong one is how a beautiful object ends up unused on a shelf because nobody is sure whether it is safe to put food in.

## What each one is

**Brass** is copper and zinc. Warm yellow, slightly golden. The everyday Indian metal — lamps, pots, urulis, plates, bells.

**Bronze** is copper and tin. Darker, browner, denser, and rings when struck. In South India this is *kansa* or *kanchu*, and it is the traditional metal for eating vessels.

**Copper** is copper. Pinkish when new, and the most reactive of the three.

**German silver** — also called nickel silver or alpaca — is copper, nickel and zinc. It contains no silver whatever, despite the name and despite being sold beside actual silver.

> If a listing says only "metal", that is not an answer. Ask which one, and be told.

## Which to use for what

**Water storage: copper.** This is the traditional use and it is what copper is genuinely good at. Fill it, leave it overnight, drink in the morning.

The caution is real, though: do not store anything acidic in copper. No lemon water, no buttermilk, no tamarind, no citrus. Acid pulls copper into the liquid, and copper is fine in trace amounts and not fine in quantity. Plain water only.

**Cooking: not bare copper or bare brass, for acidic food.** Traditional brass pots for daily cooking were tin-lined — *kalai* — and the lining is reapplied periodically. That lining exists precisely because the metal underneath reacts with food.

Unlined brass and copper are fine for dry, non-acidic uses and for short contact. They are not the vessel for a tamarind curry.

**Eating: bronze.** Kansa plates and bowls are the traditional eating vessels for good reason — bronze is less reactive than brass and considerably less than copper. Still, do not leave acidic food standing in one for hours.

**Decoration, lamps, ritual: brass.** No food contact, no worry. This is most of what is sold and it is the least complicated to own.

### The single rule worth remembering

Acid plus bare copper alloy equals a problem. Everything else in this article is detail.

## The patina question

All three darken. Copper goes brown then green-blue, brass goes dull gold then brown, bronze deepens toward near-black.

This is oxidation, it is normal, and whether you fight it is entirely aesthetic. Two schools:

**Keep it bright.** Traditional cleaning is tamarind or lemon with salt, or ash, rubbed on and rinsed thoroughly. Commercial polishes work faster. Either way you are removing a thin layer of metal every time, so a piece polished weekly for decades gets measurably thinner.

**Let it go.** A settled patina is protective, it is what antique brass looks like, and it means you never think about it again. For decorative pieces this is usually the better call.

What you should *not* do is let a lacquered piece tarnish and then polish it. Many decorative brass items are sold with a clear lacquer coat to stop tarnishing. Polish attacks the lacquer, not the metal, and leaves a patchy mess. Ask whether a piece is lacquered before you clean it.

## Spun, cast, or hand-raised

Three ways an object gets made, and it is the biggest driver of price after weight.

**Spun.** A metal disc pressed against a spinning form. Fast, uniform, thin. Most inexpensive brassware.

**Cast.** Molten metal poured into a mould. Heavy, solid, good for detailed shapes — idols, handles, bells.

**Hand-raised.** Hammered from a flat sheet over a stake, thousands of blows. You can see the hammer marks inside. Heavy, slightly irregular, expensive, and the most durable of the three.

**Dhokra** deserves its own mention: lost-wax casting, where the wax model is destroyed to release the piece. Every one is unique by necessity, with the characteristic rough, thread-like surface texture. It is one of [several crafts most people have never been shown](/blog/things-independent-makers-sell).

## Buying checks

1. **Which metal, exactly?** Brass, bronze, copper, or German silver.
2. **Is it food-safe as sold?** Lined, unlined, lacquered.
3. **How was it made?** Spun, cast, hand-raised.
4. **What is the weight?** Weight is the honest proxy for how much metal is actually there, and it is the number thin spun goods are quietest about.
5. **Is it lacquered?** Decides how you clean it forever.

## Living with it

Wash by hand, dry immediately — water sitting on brass and copper leaves marks. Nothing goes in a dishwasher; the detergent is aggressive and will strip both patina and lacquer.

Do not store food in any of them overnight, cooked food especially. Get tinning redone when you can see the copper colour coming through a lined vessel.

These objects last for generations. Almost everything that goes wrong with them comes from a few hours of acid, or a dishwasher.
