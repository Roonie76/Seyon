Here is the fact that surprises most people at the counter: in India, hallmarking silver jewellery is **voluntary**. It has been mandatory for gold since 2021, and the government has been weighing the same for silver, but as things stand a silver piece with no BIS mark on it is being sold entirely legally.

That single fact changes how you should shop for silver, because it means the responsibility for knowing what you are buying sits with you.

## What the marks mean when they are there

Two purities are recognised for silver under the current standard:

- **925 — sterling silver.** 92.5% silver, the rest usually copper. This is what jewellery is made from, because pure silver is too soft to hold a shape.
- **999 — fine silver.** 99.9% silver. Used for bullion, coins and some very simple pieces, not usually for anything that has to survive being worn.

A voluntary hallmarking scheme for silver, including HUID numbers of the kind gold carries, began operating in September 2025. So a hallmarked silver piece is possible and increasingly common — it is simply not required, and its absence is not evidence of anything.

> "925" stamped by the maker is a claim. A BIS hallmark is a verified claim. Most silver in India carries the first and not the second, and that is currently lawful.

## What to do about it

**Buy from someone who will state the purity in writing.** A message saying "this is 925 sterling silver" is worth having. It costs the seller nothing and it changes the conversation if the piece turns out to be plated.

**Learn the difference between sterling and silver-plated.** Plated jewellery is a base metal — brass, usually — with a microscopically thin silver layer. It looks identical when new. It wears through at the edges and high points within a year of regular use, revealing yellow underneath. A seller who says "silver" without saying "sterling" or "925" is often saying "plated" without saying it.

**Ask about "German silver" specifically.** German silver, also sold as nickel silver or alpaca, contains **no silver at all.** It is a copper-nickel-zinc alloy named for its colour — see [brass, bronze and copper](/blog/brass-bronze-copper-guide) for where it sits. It is a legitimate material with a long tradition in Indian jewellery and it should never be priced as silver.

**Weigh it.** Silver is sold by weight for a reason. A seller quoting a per-gram rate plus making charges is being transparent; a flat price on a heavy piece with no weight stated is worth a question.

## Oxidised, antique, tribal: what these words mean

Much of the most interesting Indian silver is deliberately darkened, and the vocabulary around it is loose.

**Oxidised** silver has been chemically darkened, and the dark is then polished off the raised surfaces so the pattern reads in contrast. It is a finish, not a material, and it is a real technique — but it is also applied to plated pieces to disguise the base metal, because a darkened surface hides wear.

**Antique finish** usually means the same thing. It rarely means old.

**Tribal silver** is a market term, not a standard. Pieces sold this way range from genuine regional work at 800–900 purity to alloy castings. Ask the purity directly.

### Turning black is normal

Silver tarnishes. It reacts with sulphur in the air, and humidity accelerates it. A piece that goes dark is behaving correctly; a piece that never does is worth a second look.

## Care that is actually necessary

- **Store it dry and sealed.** A zip bag with the air pressed out does more than any polish. Tarnish needs air.
- **Put it on last, take it off first.** Perfume, hairspray and sunscreen are what dull silver, not wearing it.
- **Polish with a proper silver cloth.** Toothpaste and baking soda are abrasive; they work by removing a layer of your jewellery.
- **Never use dips on oxidised pieces.** A chemical dip strips the darkening out of the recesses in seconds and the design disappears. This is the most common way people ruin a piece they love.
- **Wear it.** Regular contact with skin keeps silver bright. Jewellery kept in a box tarnishes faster than jewellery that is used.

## Buying from a small maker

Independent silversmiths are where the interesting work is, and the checks are the same ones with one addition: ask to see the piece from the back and the inside.

Handmade silver has evidence of being made — a soldered seam, a slight asymmetry, file marks inside a ring shank, a stamp struck by hand and therefore not perfectly level. Cast production silver is uniform everywhere, including places nobody looks.

Neither is better in the abstract. But if you are paying for handwork, the back of the piece is where you find out whether you got it — which is [most of what handmade means](/blog/what-handmade-is-allowed-to-mean) in practice.

## The short version

Silver hallmarking is voluntary, so no mark is not a red flag and a stamped "925" is only the seller's word. Get the purity in writing, learn that German silver is not silver, do not dip your oxidised pieces, and judge handwork by the side of the piece nobody was meant to see.
