This is an overview written to help you ask your accountant the right questions. It is not tax advice, the rules change, and the details of your situation matter more than any article. Where a rule is stated below, the section or notification is named so you or your accountant can check the current text.

## The two questions that decide everything

Almost every seller's confusion comes from mixing up two separate questions.

1. **Am I over the turnover threshold?**
2. **Am I selling through an e-commerce operator?**

They have different answers and different consequences, and the second one is why so much of what you read online says "GST is compulsory for online sellers from the first rupee." That statement used to be broadly true. It has not been the whole picture since October 2023.

## Question one: the turnover threshold

Under section 22 of the CGST Act, registration is required once aggregate turnover in a financial year crosses the threshold. Broadly:

- **Goods:** ₹40 lakh in most states.
- **Services:** ₹20 lakh in most states.
- **Special category states:** lower — commonly ₹20 lakh for goods and ₹10 lakh for services.

Which threshold applies to you depends on your state and on whether you supply goods, services, or both. Several states have exercised options that change these numbers. Check yours; do not assume the national figure.

**Aggregate turnover** is not the same as profit, and not the same as sales on one platform. It is computed on the PAN, across all your businesses, across India, including exempt supplies. A seller with two small side businesses can be over the line without either one feeling large.

## Question two: selling through an e-commerce operator

Section 24 of the CGST Act lists people who must register **regardless of turnover**. Clause (ix) covers persons who supply goods or services through an electronic commerce operator required to collect tax at source under section 52.

This is the clause behind the "register from rupee one" advice, and for large marketplaces that collect your money it is correct.

### The exemption most articles omit

**Notification 34/2023–Central Tax, dated 31 July 2023, in force from 1 October 2023**, waives that mandatory registration for persons supplying **goods** through an ECO, provided they are otherwise below the section 22 threshold and meet every one of these conditions:

1. No inter-state supply of goods.
2. Supply through ECOs in only one state or union territory.
3. They hold a PAN.
4. Before making any supply, they declare their PAN, business address, and the state of operation on the common portal.
5. That declaration is validated on the portal.
6. They obtain an enrolment number after successful validation.
7. Not more than one enrolment number per state or union territory.
8. No supplies are made until the enrolment number is granted.

Two limits matter most in practice. The exemption covers **goods only** — it does not extend to services. And **inter-state supply disqualifies you**, which for most sellers is the condition that bites, because shipping one order to another state ends the exemption.

> If you ship across state lines, the exemption is not available to you, whatever your turnover.

## Tax collected at source, and who it applies to

Section 52 requires an e-commerce operator to collect TCS — commonly 1% of the net value of taxable supplies, split as 0.5% CGST and 0.5% SGST, or 1% IGST on inter-state supplies — and deposit it against the seller's GSTIN, where the seller claims it back.

The mechanism assumes the operator is **collecting the consideration** for the supply. Platforms that never handle the buyer's payment sit differently under section 52 than platforms that do. Seyon does not process payments — buyers pay the seller directly — and we are not in a position to tell you what that means for your registration, because it depends on facts about your business we do not have. Ask your accountant specifically: *given that the platform does not collect the consideration, does section 24(ix) apply to me?* It is a question with a real answer, and it is not one to guess at.

## What registration actually costs you in effort

If you do register, the ongoing obligations are the part sellers underestimate:

- **GSTR-1** — outward supplies, monthly or quarterly depending on your scheme.
- **GSTR-3B** — summary return and payment, monthly or quarterly.
- **Annual return** where applicable.
- **Tax-compliant invoices** with your GSTIN, HSN codes, and the correct rate.
- **Records kept** for the prescribed period.

Missed returns attract late fees per return per day, and a lapsed filing history is harder to repair than to maintain. Budget either the time or the accountant.

### The composition scheme

Small suppliers under a turnover limit may opt for a composition scheme — a flat rate on turnover with much lighter filing, but no input tax credit and restrictions on inter-state supply and on supplying through ECOs. Whether it fits depends on your margins and your customers. It is worth asking about, and it is not automatically the cheaper option.

## What to do, practically

1. **Work out your aggregate turnover on PAN**, all businesses, this financial year to date.
2. **Check the threshold for your specific state**, for goods and for services separately.
3. **Decide whether you supply inter-state.** One order to a neighbouring state changes the answer, which makes [your shipping decisions](/blog/shipping-for-small-sellers-india) a tax question as well as a cost one.
4. **Establish whether the platforms you sell on collect payment**, and therefore whether section 52 applies to them.
5. **Take those four answers to an accountant** rather than to a comment section.

An hour with a chartered accountant costs less than a year of filing something you did not need, or of not filing something you did.

## The honest summary

If you sell goods, stay under the threshold, sell only within your own state, and your platform does not collect your money, there is a reasonable case that you are not required to register today. If any one of those is untrue, the answer probably changes.

That is precisely why this article ends with *ask someone qualified* rather than a decision. Tax rules change with each Council meeting, and being confidently wrong about GST is expensive in a way that being cautious is not.
