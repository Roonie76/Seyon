Somewhere in most Indian families there is a locker or a steel box with jewellery nobody wears. Heavy sets bought for a wedding thirty years ago. Bangles from a grandmother. A chain that is too short, earrings that are too long, a ring sized for a different hand.

It is usually treated as sentiment to be preserved or gold to be sold eventually. There is a third option that is more interesting than either.

## What is actually in the box

Before deciding anything, find out what you have. Three questions:

**What is it made of?** Old family gold is frequently 22K and sometimes higher, which is purer than most of what is sold today. Silver from the same era is often high purity too, though frequently unstamped — and [an unstamped piece is not evidence of anything either way](/blog/how-to-buy-silver-jewellery-india), because hallmarking silver in India is voluntary even now.

**What does it weigh?** A jeweller will weigh it in front of you. This number is the floor under every decision you make afterwards.

**Is any of it worth more as it is?** This is the one people get wrong. Genuinely old [temple jewellery](/blog/temple-jewellery-south-india), fine [jadau or meenakari work](/blog/kundan-polki-jadau-meenakari) with enamel on the reverse, and anything with real polki is often worth considerably more intact than melted — because the craft is worth more than the metal, and some of it is no longer made the same way.

> Melting is irreversible and it is the default advice. Get a second opinion on anything with visible handwork before anybody puts it in a crucible.

## The three things you can do

**Wear it as it is.** Underrated. A single piece from an old set, worn alone with something plain, usually looks better than the whole set worn together — because the set was designed for a wedding and you are not at one. One heavy jhumka with a cotton kurta reads as deliberate. The full parure reads as costume.

**Alter it.** The cheapest option by a distance, and the most overlooked. A goldsmith can:

- Shorten or lengthen a chain
- Resize a ring, sometimes several sizes
- Convert a long earring into a stud by removing the drop
- Turn a heavy necklace into a pendant, keeping the central motif
- Replace a broken clasp with a better one
- Convert screw-back earrings to posts, or the reverse
- Split a pair of bangles into a set of thinner ones

Most of these cost a small fraction of the piece's value and take a week.

**Remake it.** The piece is melted or dismantled and the metal becomes something new. You pay making charges on gold you already own, which is why a remade piece is dramatically cheaper than an equivalent new one.

## How remaking actually works

Three things to establish before you hand anything over.

**Weight in, weight out.** Some metal is genuinely lost in melting and working — a small percentage. Agree what that percentage is, in writing, before you start. This is the single most common dispute.

**Who holds it, and for how long.** Get a receipt describing the pieces, their weight, and the date. Photograph everything from several angles first.

**What happens to the stones.** Old stones can usually be reset. Say explicitly that you want them back whether or not they are reused.

If the piece has enamel, uncut stones or fine wirework, ask whether the goldsmith can preserve any of it rather than melting the whole thing. Sometimes a motif can be lifted out and reset into something new, which keeps the part that was actually made by hand.

## What tends to work

Old jewellery is heavy, and heaviness is the constraint. What converts well:

- **Heavy necklaces into pendants.** Keep the central motif, lose the weight.
- **Long earrings into studs or short drops.** The most common alteration and almost always an improvement in wearability.
- **Bangle sets into a single wider cuff**, or into rings.
- **A set nobody wears into three separate pieces** that each work alone.

What tends not to work: making a large old piece look contemporary by simplifying it. The proportions are from another design language and they resist. Better to keep it whole and wear it as the strong old thing it is.

## The conversation to have first

Talk to whoever the jewellery belonged to, if you can. Not for permission — for information. Where it was made, what it was for, whether anything about it was unusual. That is the part that cannot be recovered later, and it is what turns an object into something you will pass on rather than sell.

It is also, quite often, how you find out the piece is a great deal more interesting than it looks.
