The gap between a handloom sari and a powerloom sari sold as one is, at the retail counter, mostly a matter of trust. The two can look nearly identical in a photograph and the price difference can be several times over.

They are not identical in the hand, though, and the tells are learnable in an afternoon.

## What the difference actually is

A handloom is worked by a person. The shuttle is thrown across by hand or with a hand-pulled cord, and the beat that packs each weft thread into place is delivered by a human arm.

A powerloom does the same motions mechanically, faster, at constant force. A powerloom is not a moral failing — it produces enormous amounts of perfectly good cloth, and most of what everyone wears comes from one. The problem is only ever price: powerloom cloth sold as handloom.

Everything below follows from one fact. **A machine is consistent and a person is not**, and the small inconsistencies are what you are paying for.

## Six checks you can do holding the cloth

**1. Look at the reverse.** On handloom fabric, the back is nearly as tidy as the front, and extra weft threads are usually cut and left as small tufts. Powerloom work often shows long floats of thread carried across the back, or a machine-neat finish that is *too* even.

**2. Find the selvedge.** The finished edge running the length of the cloth. Handloom selvedges are slightly irregular and often a little thicker. Powerloom selvedges are dead straight, and frequently show tiny regular pinholes from the machine's gripping mechanism. Those pinholes are close to conclusive.

**3. Hold it to the light.** Handloom cloth shows minute variation in thread density — faint shadows and thicker lines where the beat came down harder or softer. Powerloom cloth is uniform across its whole width because the force never varied.

**4. Look for the mistake.** A dropped thread, a slightly wandering line in a stripe, a motif repeat that shifts by a millimetre. Handloom has these. They are not defects; they are the fingerprint. Perfectly regular repeats over six metres suggest a machine.

**5. Feel the weight and the fall.** Handloom fabric tends to be a little heavier for the same thread count and drapes with more body, because the hand-beaten weave is denser and less even. This one takes practice, and it is the check regular buyers rely on most.

**6. Crush a corner in your fist.** Handloom cotton and silk crease and recover in a characteristic soft way. Heavy synthetic content springs back too readily — useful because a great deal of "silk" sold cheaply is polyester.

> The rule is simple to remember. Perfection is a machine. You are looking for controlled imperfection.

## The marks that mean something

**The Handloom Mark** is a government-backed label certifying that a fabric is handwoven. **Silk Mark** certifies pure natural silk. **India Handloom Brand** is a stricter mark covering quality and defect standards as well as origin.

Two honest caveats. These marks are not universal — a great many genuine weavers, especially individuals selling directly, are not registered and never will be. And a label sewn onto a garment is only as good as the supply chain behind it.

So the marks are useful evidence and poor proof. The physical checks above are what you actually rely on when buying from an individual.

## Geographical indication is not a quality mark

A GI tag — Chanderi, Maheshwari, Kota Doria, Pochampally, Kanjeevaram and many others — says a product genuinely comes from where it claims. It is about origin, not about handwork or quality.

This trips people up constantly. It is entirely possible for a GI-tagged fabric to be powerloom woven in the correct town. [Six weaves worth knowing by name](/blog/indian-weaves-worth-knowing) covers what the names themselves tell you.

## Questions worth asking a seller

1. **Handloom or powerloom?** Ask it plainly. A direct seller who answers "powerloom, and priced accordingly" has just told you a great deal about their honesty.
2. **What is the fibre, exactly?** Cotton, silk, a blend, and in what proportion.
3. **Who wove it, and where?** The best answers are specific: a weaver's name, a cluster, a village.
4. **Is the dye natural or chemical?** Both are fine. Natural dye behaves differently over time and needs to be washed differently — the same is true of [block-printed cloth](/blog/block-print-vs-screen-print).

## Living with handloom

Wash it separately the first three times — handloom, especially naturally dyed, will release colour. Cold water, mild detergent, no wringing, dry in shade. Fold along different lines each time you store it, because silk breaks along a permanent crease.

Expect it to change. Handloom cotton gets softer for years. That is the argument for it over anything else you could have bought.
