Setting up from nothing happens for a lot of reasons — a first flat, a move to a new city, a marriage, the end of one, a fresh start after something difficult. The reasons are different and the shopping problem is identical: an empty space, a finite budget, and pressure to fill it quickly.

Filling it quickly is the mistake. Here is a better order.

## The rule: buy what you touch, defer what you look at

The things used several times a day should be bought properly and first. The things that are only looked at can wait, and waiting improves them, because you will find better ones once you know the space.

This inverts the usual sequence, which is furniture and decoration first and the daily objects picked up cheaply at the end.

> An empty shelf is better than a shelf filled with things chosen to fill it. You will find the right object eventually, and the gap costs nothing in the meantime.

## Week one: the twelve things

The genuinely irreducible list, in rough order:

1. **Bedding.** Cotton. You use it eight hours a day.
2. **Two lamps.** Not the ceiling light. This changes a room more than any other purchase and [it is the most under-bought thing in Indian homes](/blog/what-makes-a-house-feel-like-home).
3. **One good knife**, if you cook at all.
4. **A pan and a pot.**
5. **Two plates, two bowls, two mugs.** Two, not eight. You will find out what you need.
6. **A kettle or a vessel for boiling water.**
7. **Towels.**
8. **A dustbin, a bucket, cleaning things.** Unglamorous and instantly necessary.
9. **Something on the floor.** A dhurrie or mat defines a room faster than anything on a wall.
10. **A place to put keys and post.** A bowl or a tray. Prevents a specific daily irritation.
11. **A drying rack.**
12. **One plant.**

That is a functioning home. Everything after this is improvement rather than necessity, which means it can be bought slowly and well.

## What to buy well immediately

Three exceptions to buying cheap at the start, because the cheap version fails and gets replaced within a year:

**Bedding.** Cotton, and enough of it to change. Synthetic bedding is the most common false economy in setting up.

**The knife.** One good one beats a block of bad ones, and it lasts decades.

**The mug or cup you drink from every morning.** Trivial-sounding and consistently reported as the best small purchase anyone makes. [Stoneware survives daily use](/blog/terracotta-stoneware-porcelain) and chips rather than shattering.

## What to deliberately defer

**Furniture beyond a bed and somewhere to sit.** You do not yet know how the space is used. Buying a sofa in week one is how people end up with a sofa in the wrong place for six years.

**Anything decorative.** The room will tell you what it needs after a month of living in it.

**Sets of anything.** Buy two of everything, find out what you actually run out of, then buy more.

**Matching.** A room furnished all at once looks furnished all at once. The rooms that feel like someone's have accumulated.

## Where secondhand and inherited genuinely win

If there is family furniture, brass, or kitchen equipment available, take it before buying new. Not for sentiment — because old household equipment is frequently better made than its replacement, and [a great deal of what was cleared out in the last few decades is worth having back](/blog/your-mothers-house-looks-good-now).

Brass and bronze vessels, cast iron, stone mortars, cane baskets, steel storage. All of it outlasts the new equivalent and most of it can be restored — tinning redone, cane rewoven, cast iron reseasoned.

## The budget-order that works

If money is tight, and it usually is:

1. **Function first, at whatever quality is affordable.** A working kitchen beats a beautiful empty one.
2. **Then upgrade the three or four things you touch most**, one at a time, over months.
3. **Then, and only then, the things that are looked at.**

Step two is where most of the eventual satisfaction is, and it is the step people skip in favour of step three.

## If the fresh start is a difficult one

One practical note, offered plainly. There is a strong pull to either buy nothing at all or to buy a great deal quickly, and both tend to be regretted.

The steadier approach is the twelve things above, then a deliberate pause, then one considered object — something made by a person, chosen slowly, that marks the new place as yours rather than as a replacement for the old one. [That single object does more than a room full of furnishing](/blog/what-makes-a-house-feel-like-home), and choosing it slowly is part of what it is for.

There is no rush. An unfinished room in a place that is yours is a better outcome than a completed room assembled in a fortnight.
