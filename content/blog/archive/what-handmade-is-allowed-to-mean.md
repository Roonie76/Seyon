"Handmade" is doing an enormous amount of work in online retail and almost none of it is regulated. Unlike a gold hallmark, which has a standard and an enforcement body behind it, the word handmade on a listing is a claim made by the person who wants your money.

That is not an argument for cynicism. It is an argument for knowing what the word can honestly stretch to cover, so you can ask a better question than "is this really handmade?"

## The spectrum, honestly

Almost nothing is made entirely by hand from raw material, and almost nothing sold as handmade is entirely machine-made either. Real production sits somewhere along a line:

**Hand-made throughout.** One person, hand tools, from material to finished object. A wheel-thrown pot, a hand-raised copper vessel, a hand-woven length of cloth. Slow, expensive, and the variation between pieces is unmistakable.

**Hand-worked on a machine.** A potter's wheel is a machine. So is a handloom, a sewing machine, a lathe. The skill and the pace are entirely human; the tool is mechanical. This is where most of what people mean by handmade actually lives, and it is completely legitimate.

**Hand-finished.** The body is produced industrially and a person does the part that shows — painting, embroidery, assembly, carving. A block-printed panel sewn onto machine-woven cloth. Real handwork on a manufactured base.

**Hand-assembled.** Bought components put together by a person. Common in jewellery, and honest when described as such.

**Hand-packed.** Someone put it in a box. This is sometimes what "handmade with love" means.

> None of the first four are dishonest. The fifth pretending to be the first is.

## Better questions than "is it handmade"

The word is too blunt to be useful. These are not:

1. **Which part is done by hand?** A good maker answers immediately and specifically, because it is what they are proud of. "I throw and glaze everything; the clay is commercially prepared."
2. **How long does one take?** Time is the honest proxy for handwork, and makers know their numbers.
3. **How many can you make in a week?** If the answer is four hundred, one pair of hands did not make them.
4. **Will two be identical?** "Yes, exactly" and "handmade" are difficult to hold together.
5. **Do you make it, or do you work with makers?** Both are fine. A shop that designs and commissions from a workshop is doing something valuable. It is a different thing from a solo maker, and the price should reflect which.

## What variation should look like

The single most reliable signal is that pieces differ from each other in ways that a machine would not produce, in places a machine would not vary.

Slight differences in height between [two mugs](/blog/terracotta-stoneware-porcelain). A [block print repeat that steps](/blog/block-print-vs-screen-print). Hammer marks inside a copper pot. File marks inside a ring shank. A [weave that is very slightly denser](/blog/handloom-vs-powerloom) in one section.

What is *not* evidence of handwork: a rough finish. Poor work is poor work in any process, and "it's handmade" has become the standard excuse for a badly finished object. Genuine handmade should be *better* finished than production in the places that matter, because a person was looking at it.

## The reseller question

A large share of "handmade" listings online are resold wholesale goods. Nothing about that is illegal and some of it is well curated.

Two tells. **Identical photographs** — reverse image search a listing photo and see how many other shops are using it. And **breadth without depth** — one seller offering brassware, ceramics, textiles, jewellery and candles is a shop, not a workshop, and is very unlikely to have made any of it.

Again: shops are fine. Shops charging workshop prices while implying they made it are not.

## What you are actually paying for

It is worth being clear-eyed about this, because "support artisans" is asked to justify a lot of prices.

You are paying for time, for skill that took years to acquire, for materials bought in small quantities at bad rates, and for a short chain between the maker and you. You are also paying for the fact that nobody else will have exactly this one.

You are *not* paying for it to be better in every measurable way. Machine production is more consistent, more precisely toleranced, and cheaper. If uniformity is what you want, buy the uniform thing without guilt.

The case for handmade is that some qualities — variation, evidence of a person, materials chosen rather than costed, an object that can be repaired — do not survive scale. If those are what you want, they are only available this way.

## The one-line test

Ask the seller a specific question about how the thing was made.

Someone who made it will answer at length and possibly at more length than you wanted. Someone who did not will answer vaguely, or pleasantly, or about something else. It is a remarkably reliable test and it costs one message.
