Most jewellery traditions are about the wearer. Temple jewellery began as something else entirely: ornament made for deities, and later for the dancers who performed before them. That origin is why it still looks unlike anything else being sold.

## What makes it recognisable

Temple jewellery is built around a small set of motifs that repeat across centuries — the *lakshmi* face, the peacock, the mango or paisley, the lotus, the *rudraksha* bead, the coiled snake.

Two features give it its distinctive weight and depth. **Repoussé work**, where the design is raised by hammering the metal from behind, so the figures stand out in real relief rather than being engraved into a flat surface. And **kemp stones** — the opaque deep red and green stones, set in raised collars, that read from a distance under stage light.

Kemp is not ruby and was never pretending to be. It is a distinct material with its own tradition, chosen because it is dense, matte and legible from thirty feet away.

> It was designed to be seen from the back of a hall, by lamplight. That is why the shapes are large, the relief is deep and the colours are flat and strong.

## The forms, and where they sit

- **Vanki** — the angled armband worn on the upper arm, usually an inverted V.
- **Oddiyanam** — the waist belt, the heaviest single piece, worn over a drape.
- **Jhumka** — the bell-shaped drop earring, the form that escaped into general use.
- **Maang tikka / nethi chutti** — the forehead ornament, with side chains in the dance version.
- **Haram and manga malai** — the long necklaces; manga malai is the one built from linked mango motifs.
- **Kammal and mattal** — the ear stud and the chain that carries its weight to the hair, which is the practical solution to a heavy earring.

Bharatanatyam costume uses most of these together, which is where many people first see them.

## What it is actually made of

Three tiers, all sold as temple jewellery, and the difference in price is very large.

**Gold.** The traditional and the heirloom version. Hallmarked if you buy it properly — a hallmarked gold piece in India carries the BIS triangle, the purity and fineness, and a six-character HUID you can verify yourself in the BIS Care app.

**[Silver](/blog/how-to-buy-silver-jewellery-india), gold-plated.** Sterling silver worked in the same techniques and then plated. This is the sensible middle, and a great deal of the best contemporary work is made this way — genuine repoussé, real weight, a fraction of the cost. Ask for the silver purity and the plating thickness; heavier plating in microns lasts longer.

**Base metal, gold-plated.** Brass or an alloy, plated. Perfectly reasonable as costume jewellery for a performance or an occasion. It should never carry a silver or gold price.

### The one question that separates them

Ask what the **base metal** is, in writing, before anything about the plating. Sellers describing a piece only as "gold plated temple jewellery" are describing the surface, and the surface is the cheap part.

## Cast, or actually worked

Much of what is sold now is cast — molten metal into a mould, which reproduces a shape quickly and cheaply. Genuine repoussé is hammered by hand and takes days.

You can usually tell by looking at the back. Hand-raised relief leaves a hollow behind every raised element; the reverse is a negative of the front. A cast piece has a flat or roughly filled back, sometimes with a visible sprue mark where metal entered the mould.

Neither is wrong. But a cast piece at hand-worked prices is a common and expensive substitution.

## Wearing it now

The interesting development of the last decade is that these forms have moved off the stage. A single temple jhumka with a plain cotton sari, one vanki worn as a cuff, a manga malai over a plain kurta — the pieces are strong enough to be the only thing you are wearing, and they read as deliberate rather than costume.

Some practical notes:

- **Weight is real.** A full oddiyanam is heavy enough to need the right drape to carry it. Try before an all-day event.
- **Ear support matters.** For heavy jhumkas, the *mattal* chain is not decorative; it transfers the weight to the hair. Modern equivalents exist.
- **Kemp is not hard.** The stones chip if knocked and the settings can loosen. This is not everyday jewellery.

## Care

Plated pieces have a finite life on the surface — perfume, sweat and friction all take it off, and the highest points wear first. Put it on last, wipe it after wearing with a dry cloth, and store each piece in its own soft pouch so stones do not scratch neighbouring metal.

Never dip plated work in silver cleaner. It strips the plating and the darkened recesses in one go, and there is no undoing it.

Keep the boxes. Temple jewellery is one of the few categories where the piece is often worth more in twenty years than it cost, and provenance helps.
