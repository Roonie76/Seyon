Most online shopping is a search box, which means you can only find things you already know the name of. That is a narrow way to discover anything, and it is why so much of what independent Indian makers produce stays invisible to people who would happily buy it.

What follows is a list of things that exist, that are made by hand, and that most people have never thought to look for.

## Eleven things worth knowing exist

**Bidri ware.** Blackened zinc alloy inlaid with silver wire, from Bidar in Karnataka. The black is not paint — it comes from a soil treatment that darkens the alloy and leaves the silver bright, so the pattern seems to float. Boxes, vases, cufflinks, bangles.

**[Channapatna toys](/blog/indian-lacquerware-channapatna).** Turned wood, coloured with vegetable dyes and polished with a leaf so the surface is lacquered rather than painted. Traditionally made from *Wrightia tinctoria*, the ivory-wood, though other woods are used now. They are safe for a child to put in their mouth, which is exactly why adults buy them for shelves.

**Kantha throws.** Old cotton saris layered and quilted together with a running stitch, originally so that nothing was wasted. The result is soft in a way new cloth cannot be, because the fabric has already been washed a hundred times.

**Dhokra.** Cast metal made by the lost-wax method, unchanged for several thousand years. Every piece is one of one, because the mould is destroyed to release it. The finish is deliberately rough and the shapes are unmistakable.

**Longpi pottery.** Black stoneware from Manipur, made without a wheel, from weathered rock and serpentinite ground together. It goes on a flame. Most black pottery is a glaze; this is the material itself.

**Sujni and Sozni embroidery.** Two entirely different traditions that both reward a magnifying glass. Sozni, from Kashmir, is fine enough that a good shawl is worked for months and reads the same on both faces.

**Copper bottles, seriously made.** Not the machine-spun ones. Hand-raised copper, hammered, with a visible seam and a weight that surprises people.

**Palm-leaf etching.** Line drawing incised into dried palm leaf and rubbed with pigment, from Odisha. It is the format manuscripts were written in, adapted to pictures.

**Naga and Kotpad textiles.** Kotpad is dyed with the root of the *aal* tree and nothing else — the colour range is small, deep and completely natural.

**Ittar.** Traditional distilled perfume, oil-based rather than alcohol-based, from Kannauj. It behaves differently from a spray: it warms on the skin and lasts, and a very small bottle goes a long way.

**Handmade paper with things in it.** Cotton-rag paper from Sanganer and elsewhere, made with petals, grass, even elephant dung. It takes ink beautifully and it is not made from trees.

> The common thread is not nostalgia. Every one of these is a material or a method that industrial production cannot reproduce cheaply, which is precisely why it survived.

## Why you have not seen most of these

Not because they are rare. Bidri and Channapatna are made in quantity by hundreds of families. You have not seen them because the people who make them sell in physical markets, through cooperatives, or through a phone number, and none of that is searchable.

An enormous amount of Indian craft is one step from being findable and has never taken that step. The maker has the skill, the stock and the photographs. What they usually do not have is a page with a price on it.

### What that means for you as a buyer

Two practical consequences.

The first is that price is often startlingly low relative to the work, because it has not passed through four hands on the way to you. The second is that you will frequently be talking directly to the person who made the object, which changes what you can ask for — a size, a colour, a second one to match.

## How to buy any of them without getting it wrong

The categories above vary enormously in quality, and the variation is not always visible in a photograph. Four questions cover most of it:

1. **Who made this, and where?** A specific answer — a name, a town — is a good sign. A vague one is not.
2. **What is it made of, exactly?** "Brass" is an answer. "Metal" is not — and [brass, bronze and copper](/blog/brass-bronze-copper-guide) are not interchangeable.
3. **How big is it?** In centimetres. Scale is the single most common disappointment in craft buying.
4. **What will it do over time?** Copper darkens. Lacquer scratches. Natural dye softens. Any of that is fine if you know it is coming.

A maker who answers all four quickly is a maker who has thought about the person receiving the parcel.

## Where to start, if you are starting

Buy one small thing from one person and see how it goes. A Channapatna toy, a kantha throw, a bottle of ittar — all of them are inexpensive enough to be a low-risk introduction and good enough that the first one usually is not the last.

It is also worth knowing [what the word handmade is allowed to cover](/blog/what-handmade-is-allowed-to-mean) before you pay a premium for it.

The pleasure of this kind of shopping is not that it is virtuous. It is that the objects are better, more interesting, and more particular than what you would otherwise have bought, and that somebody specific is glad you did.
