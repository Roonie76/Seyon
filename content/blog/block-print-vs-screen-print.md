Hand block printing is one of the few crafts where the evidence of the hand is not subtle. Once you know where to look, you can tell a block print from a screen print across a room, and you will never quite unsee it.

The difference matters because the price gap is large and the photographs are identical.

## How each one is made

**Hand block printing** uses a carved wooden block, one block per colour. The printer dips it, positions it by eye against the previous impression, and strikes it with the heel of the hand. A six-metre length with three colours means several thousand individual placements.

**Screen printing** pushes ink through a mesh stencil with a squeegee. One pass covers a large area, registration is mechanical, and a length that takes a block printer two days takes minutes.

**Digital printing** sprays the design directly onto cloth like an inkjet printer. Unlimited colours, photographic detail, no repeat limit.

All three are legitimate. Only one of them is being sold at handwork prices while being something else.

## The five tells

**1. Registration drift.** This is the big one. Where two colours meet in a block print, they will not meet perfectly every time — a hairline of overlap here, a whisker of white gap there, varying across the cloth. Screen print registration is machine-consistent; the same joint looks the same everywhere.

**2. The join line.** A block is typically 15 to 25 centimetres across, so it has to be placed again and again down the length. Look for the faint seam where one impression ends and the next begins, repeating at regular intervals. Follow a horizontal line in the pattern across the width — in a block print it will step very slightly.

**3. Ink texture.** Block-printed colour is uneven within a single shape: denser where the hand pressed harder, lighter at the edges of the block, sometimes with a faint grain from the wood. Screen ink is flat and even, and often sits on top of the fabric as a slightly rubbery film you can feel with a fingernail.

**4. The reverse.** Block printing uses thin dyes that penetrate, so the pattern is visible on the back, softer but clearly there. Many screen and most digital prints are markedly paler on the reverse, sometimes almost invisible.

**5. Colour count.** Block printing needs a separate carved block per colour, so traditional block prints tend toward two to five colours. Photographic gradients and dozens of shades mean digital.

> Every check reduces to the same question: does this design know it was placed by a hand? Drift, unevenness and a repeat that steps are what you are paying for.

## The regional styles, briefly

**Bagru**, near Jaipur, works in earthy tones — black from iron and jaggery, red from madder, with resist and mud-resist printing over a dyed ground.

**Sanganer**, also near Jaipur, prints fine floral work in bright colours on white or very pale ground, and is known for delicate blocks.

**Ajrakh**, from Kachchh and Sindh, is the deep and demanding one: complex geometric designs in indigo and madder built through many stages of resist and dyeing, printed on both faces so the cloth is reversible. Genuine Ajrakh is a weeks-long process and the price reflects it.

**Bagh**, from Madhya Pradesh, uses a distinctive red and black on white, with the cloth washed in the river as part of the process.

**Dabu** is a mud-resist technique rather than a place — a paste of clay, gum and chaff blocks the dye, then is washed off.

### One honest note about "hand block printed"

The phrase is not legally protected in retail the way a hallmark is. It appears on a great deal of screen-printed cloth. The physical checks are your protection, not the label — the same situation as [handloom against powerloom](/blog/handloom-vs-powerloom).

## What to ask

1. **Block, screen, or digital?** Asked directly, most sellers answer honestly.
2. **How many blocks?** A block printer knows the number instantly, because it is the cost of the design.
3. **Natural or chemical dye?** Both are used in genuine block printing.
4. **Will it bleed?** Naturally dyed block print usually will, for the first several washes. Anyone claiming otherwise is either using chemical dye or being optimistic.

## Care

Wash separately in cold water for at least the first three washes, and expect colour in the water — with natural dye that is the process working, not a fault. Mild detergent, no soaking, no bleach, dry in shade. Iron on the reverse.

Block print fades. It fades beautifully, into the soft chalky tones that people spend money trying to reproduce, and it does it faster if you leave it in the sun. That is worth knowing before you hang your favourite piece in a bright window.
