Some Indian crafts are having a moment. Block print is everywhere, dhokra shows up in every design shop, Channapatna is in every gift guide including ours.

Others are just as good and almost invisible — not because they are worse, but because nothing put them in front of anyone. These are the ones worth knowing about.

## Nine that deserve more attention

**Bidri.** Blackened zinc alloy inlaid with silver wire, from Bidar in Karnataka. The black comes from a soil treatment that darkens the alloy while leaving the silver bright, so the pattern appears to float. Boxes, vases, cufflinks, bangles. It is one of the most striking metal traditions anywhere and most people have never seen a piece.

**Longpi.** Black stoneware from Manipur, made without a wheel from weathered rock and serpentinite ground together. It goes directly on a flame — most black pottery is a glaze, this is the material itself. [Genuinely flame-safe stoneware is rare](/blog/terracotta-stoneware-porcelain) and this is it.

**Kotpad.** Cotton from Odisha dyed with the root of the *aal* tree and nothing else. The palette is small — deep maroons, browns, black — and completely natural. Restrained in a way most handloom is not.

**Etikoppaka.** Turned and lacquered wood from Andhra Pradesh, dyed with colours from seeds, roots, bark and leaves. More figurative than [Channapatna's forms](/blog/indian-lacquerware-channapatna), warmer in palette, and closely associated with a revival of natural dyeing after chemical colours had largely taken over.

**Sozni.** Needle embroidery from Kashmir, fine enough that a good shawl takes months and reads the same on both faces. It rewards a magnifying glass in a way very little else does.

**Palm-leaf etching.** Line drawing incised into dried palm leaf and rubbed with pigment, from Odisha — the format manuscripts were written in, adapted to pictures.

**Handmade paper.** Cotton-rag paper from Sanganer and elsewhere, made with petals, grass, even elephant dung. It takes ink beautifully and it is not made from trees.

**Ittar.** Oil-based distilled perfume from Kannauj. It behaves nothing like a spray — it warms on the skin and lasts, and a very small bottle goes a long way.

**Kansa.** Bronze eating vessels, largely displaced by steel. [Bronze is less reactive than brass](/blog/brass-bronze-copper-guide) and was the traditional eating metal for exactly that reason.

## Why these particular ones went quiet

It is rarely quality, and it is rarely that nobody makes them any more. Four recurring reasons:

**They were replaced by something cheaper and lighter.** Steel and plastic did to brass, bronze and clay what they did everywhere. The replacement was genuinely more convenient and genuinely worse at lasting.

**They need explaining.** A block print sells itself in a photograph. Bidri looks like a dark metal box until someone tells you the black is soil chemistry and the silver is inlaid wire. Crafts that need a sentence lose to crafts that do not.

**They are made in places without a market.** Manipur, Bidar, Kannauj, rural Odisha. Distance from buyers has killed more crafts than lack of skill ever did.

**The maker is unfindable.** This is the big one. The family still makes it, still has stock, still has photographs. What they do not have is a page with a price on it that a stranger can search for. [An enormous amount of Indian craft is one step from being findable and has never taken that step.](/blog/things-independent-makers-sell)

> None of these crafts died. They stopped being discoverable, which from a buyer's side looks identical.

## What a comeback actually requires

Not preservation in the museum sense. Three practical things:

**Somebody buying it at a price that works.** A craft survives on orders, not admiration.

**Adaptation to how people live now.** Channapatna's revival came partly from making objects for adults' shelves as well as children's hands. Kansa returned partly as serveware rather than daily plates. The crafts that come back are the ones that found a current use.

**Being findable.** Which is mostly a matter of a photograph, a price, and a page.

## What to buy first, if you are curious

The low-risk introductions, roughly in ascending price:

1. **A bottle of ittar.** Small, inexpensive, completely unlike anything mass-market.
2. **Handmade paper notebooks.**
3. **An Etikoppaka piece.** Charming, cheap, and safe for children.
4. **A Longpi bowl.** Unmistakable, and it will go on a flame.
5. **A small Bidri box.** The one that will get asked about.

For any of them, the four questions that cover most craft buying: who made it and where, what exactly it is made of, how big it is in centimetres, and what it will do over time. [A maker who answers all four quickly is one who has thought about the person opening the parcel.](/blog/buying-from-an-independent-maker)
