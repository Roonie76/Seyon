There is a category of gift that survives every move, every clear-out and every change of taste. It is not the expensive category and it is not the sentimental one. It is a fairly specific set of properties, and once you can name them you can buy them at any budget.

## What kept things have in common

Go through what anyone has held onto for a decade and the same qualities recur.

**It is used.** Almost nothing purely decorative survives twenty years of moving house. The things that stay are the things that got picked up — a bowl, a bangle, a shawl, a box, a lamp. Use is what earns shelf space permanently.

**It improves.** Silver that has been worn smooth. Cotton that has softened. Brass that has darkened into a real patina. Wood that has taken on a hand polish. Materials that age *upward* are kept; materials that degrade are eventually thrown out with a small guilt.

**It has no expiry of taste.** Not trend-driven, not tied to a year. Plain forms outlive decorated ones for exactly this reason.

**It is repairable.** A clasp can be replaced, a rim re-tinned, a seam restitched. Anything that cannot be mended is on a clock.

**It carries a story someone can retell.** Where it came from, who made it, why you chose it. This is the one people underestimate, and it costs nothing.

> The things people keep are usually not the most expensive things they were given. They are the things that were used, that got better, and that had a sentence attached.

## The categories that reliably survive

**A single piece of jewellery in a plain form.** Not a set, not a statement piece. A plain band, small hoops, one bangle. [The daily-wear rules apply](/blog/everyday-jewellery-that-lasts) — low profile, a closure that works, solid metal rather than plating. Plated jewellery is the least keepable gift in this category, because it looks worst exactly where it is most seen.

**One vessel that gets used.** A hand-thrown mug or bowl. If someone drinks from it every morning it becomes theirs in a way no ornament does. [Stoneware is the material that survives daily use](/blog/terracotta-stoneware-porcelain); it chips rather than shattering.

**A textile that is not clothing.** A kantha throw, a block-printed bedcover, a good shawl. No sizing problem, no taste risk in the way a garment carries, and handloom cotton genuinely improves for years.

**Brass or bronze for the home.** A small uruli, a diya, a bowl. These outlive everyone and they look better at twenty years than at one. [Which metal for which purpose is worth getting right](/blog/brass-bronze-copper-guide) before you buy something that turns out not to be safe for food.

**A box.** Underrated entirely. Everyone needs somewhere to put things, boxes are used constantly, and a well-made wooden, brass or Bidri box is kept forever without any obligation to display it.

## What does not survive

**Anything plated.** It wears through and then it is a thing you cannot wear and cannot throw away.

**Scented anything.** Personal, frequently unwelcome, and consumed or discarded within a year either way.

**Sets.** One element breaks or gets lost and the rest become a reproach.

**Sized things.** Rings and clothes. Even correct at the time, bodies change.

**Anything requiring maintenance the person will not do.** Unlacquered brass that needs polishing, silk that needs particular washing. Give the care instruction with it or buy something else.

**Very large decorative objects.** They commit a wall or a floor, and the first move ends them.

## The part that costs nothing

Write down where it came from.

Not a card with a message — a line about the object. *"This is Channapatna work. The colour is lac, melted onto the wood by friction while it spins on a lathe, and it is coloured with vegetable dye because these were made as toys for children."*

That sentence is the difference between an object and an object with provenance, and provenance is most of what makes something get kept. It is also the thing that gets repeated when someone asks about it, which is how a gift stays associated with the giver.

## If you want one rule

Buy something small, plain, made of a material that ages well, that will be picked up several times a week — and tell them what it is.

That will be in the house in fifteen years. The impressive thing bought at four times the price, quite often, will not.
