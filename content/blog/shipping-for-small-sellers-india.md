Shipping is where most small sellers lose money without noticing, because the losses arrive one order at a time and each one feels like an exception.

The decisions below are worth making once, deliberately, before the orders start — not improvised per buyer at 11pm.

## Decide who pays, and say so

There are three honest positions. Pick one and put it on the page.

**The buyer pays actual shipping.** Cleanest and fairest, worst for conversion. A cost that appears late in a conversation feels like a penalty.

**You absorb it and price it in.** "Free delivery across India" is popular for a reason: buyers respond to a single number. It only works if you have actually [added the average shipping cost to the item price](/blog/how-to-price-handmade-products), and it punishes you on the heaviest, furthest orders.

**A flat rate, with a threshold.** "₹80 across India, free above ₹1,500." Predictable for the buyer, roughly self-correcting for you, and it nudges order size up. For most small sellers this is the sensible default.

What does not work is deciding per order. It is slow, it is inconsistent between buyers, and the buyer who paid ₹140 will eventually find out about the buyer who paid nothing.

> Shipping that is decided in the chat is shipping you will get wrong in your favour rarely and against yourself often.

## Know your real cost before you quote

Courier pricing in India is driven by four things, and sellers routinely discover the third and fourth only after a bad month.

1. **Weight.** Priced in slabs, usually starting at 500g and stepping up.
2. **Volumetric weight.** Length × breadth × height divided by a divisor set by the courier. Whichever is greater — actual or volumetric — is what you are charged. This is why a light, bulky item costs more to send than a heavy, small one.
3. **Zone.** Within-city, within-state, metro-to-metro, rest of India, and remote. The spread between the cheapest and most expensive zone is large.
4. **Mode.** Prepaid versus cash-on-delivery, which usually carries an additional handling fee and a percentage.

Weigh and measure your three most common packed orders — packed, with the box and the filler — and get real quotes for a near zone and a far one. Those numbers are your flat rate. Guessing produces a rate that is either uncompetitive or quietly loss-making.

## Cash on delivery: the decision that matters most

COD is still expected by a large share of Indian buyers, and refusing it costs sales. It also carries real costs that a small seller feels sharply:

- A handling fee plus a percentage of the order value.
- **Return-to-origin.** The buyer refuses or is unreachable, and you pay freight both ways for an order that earned you nothing.
- Money that reaches you a week or more after delivery.
- No protection against the order that was never seriously intended.

A middle path that works well at small scale: offer prepaid as the default with a small incentive, and offer COD with a partial advance — enough to cover your two-way freight — for buyers who want it. Most genuine buyers accept this without argument, and the ones who will not are frequently the ones who would have refused delivery.

## Packing, which is cheaper than replacing

The rule is that the parcel will be dropped, stacked under something heavier, and left in the rain for twenty minutes.

- **Fragile items need to not touch the box.** Two centimetres of filler on every side, including the bottom. Wrap first, then fill.
- **Tape every seam**, including the ones that look closed.
- **Waterproof the inner layer**, not just the outer. A plastic sleeve inside a cardboard box survives what a taped box does not.
- **Put the address inside as well as outside.** Labels come off.
- **Nothing loose that can shift.** Movement is what breaks things, not impact.

The cost of packing properly is a few rupees per order. The cost of a broken item is the item, the freight both ways, the replacement, and a buyer who tells people.

### Weigh the packed parcel, not the product

Sellers quote from the product weight and get billed on the packed weight. On light items the packaging is frequently half the total.

## What to tell the buyer, and when

Three messages, and they prevent most of the anxious follow-ups:

1. **On order:** what they bought, the amount, the address you are shipping to, and when it will leave. Ask them to confirm the address in that message — this is the cheapest error-prevention available.
2. **On dispatch:** courier name, tracking number, and an honest arrival window. Say "4 to 7 days", not "2 days" that you hope for.
3. **On delivery:** a short check-in asking whether it arrived intact. If it did not, [your returns policy](/blog/returns-and-refunds-small-business) should already say what happens next.

Over-promising delivery dates is the most common self-inflicted wound in small-scale shipping. A buyer told seven days who receives it in five is delighted. The same buyer told two days is angry on day three, about the identical parcel.

## Keep a record you can search

Date, buyer, city, courier, tracking number, weight, what you were charged, what you collected. A spreadsheet is enough.

After thirty orders it tells you your true average shipping cost, which zone hurts, whether COD is paying for itself, and which courier actually delivers on time in your area. Until you have that, every shipping decision you make is a guess, including the one you are currently confident about.
