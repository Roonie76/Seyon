Someone finds you through a reel, a friend's story, or a hashtag they were not really searching. They like the work. Then they have to figure out whether you are a business or a person who occasionally sells things, and whether asking the price is going to turn into a negotiation they did not want.

That gap — between interest and asking — is where most Instagram selling in India quietly fails. Not at the product. Not at the price. At the moment someone decides it is too much effort to find out.

## Start with the account, not the content

Before you post anything else, three things on your profile do more work than a month of reels.

- **A bio that says what you sell and where you ship from.** "Handmade brass homeware · Made in Coimbatore · Ships across India" tells a stranger more than any tagline. Location matters more than sellers expect; a buyer in Chennai reads "Coimbatore" as *this will arrive in three days*.
- **A pinned post with prices.** Not a catalogue. Three or four current items with numbers beside them. This single post ends more silent scrolling than anything else you will do.
- **A link that goes somewhere with prices on it.** If your link goes to a homepage, a Linktree of five options, or nowhere, you have added a step to a person who was ready.

> Most of what people call "low engagement" is actually unanswered questions. The buyer did not lose interest. They ran out of ways to find out.

## Switch to a professional account, then ignore most of what it tells you

A professional account (Business or Creator) is free and gives you three things worth having: contact buttons, the ability to run promotions, and Insights.

Insights will show you a lot of numbers. Almost all of them are noise at your size. Two are not:

1. **Profile visits** — how many people wanted to know more about you.
2. **Link taps or DM starts** — how many of those tried to buy.

The ratio between those two is your actual problem statement. If a hundred people visited your profile and two messaged you, the issue is your profile, not your reach. If four hundred people saw the post and eleven visited the profile, the issue is the post.

Chasing follower count instead of that ratio is the most common expensive mistake in social selling. Ten thousand followers who cannot find your prices are worth less than four hundred who can.

## What to post, in the order that works

The instinct is to post finished products. Finished products are the least interesting thing you make, because a photograph of a finished product looks like every other photograph of a finished product.

What actually earns a follow, roughly in order of effectiveness:

1. **The work in progress.** Hands, tools, mess, the half-made thing. This is the content that makes someone believe a person made it, and it is the strongest asset a shop with no reviews yet has — more on that in [how to look legitimate when nobody has heard of you](/blog/how-to-look-legitimate-as-a-new-seller).
2. **The thing at scale.** Forty of the same item laid out. It reads as competence.
3. **A packed order leaving.** Proof that other people buy from you, without a single word of self-promotion.
4. **The finished product, well lit.** Necessary, but it converts people who are already convinced.
5. **Anything with your face and voice in it.** Uncomfortable, and it works. People buy from people, especially in a market where a stranger taking your money is a real risk.

### The caption does one job

End every selling post with the same two lines: the price, and exactly how to buy. Not "DM for price" — the price. "₹1,450 · DM the word BRASS and I'll send the link" works because the buyer knows what happens next and what it costs before they commit to a conversation.

Hiding the price feels like it creates conversation. It mostly creates silence with extra steps.

## Reels, honestly

Reels are the only reliable way to reach people who do not follow you, which makes them the only real discovery channel Instagram gives a small seller. That is the whole argument for them.

They are also unpredictable, unrepeatable, and disappear. A reel that reaches 80,000 people gives you a very good week. It does not give you a business, because you cannot make it happen again on purpose, and in six months nobody can find it.

Treat reels as the top of the funnel and something durable — a storefront, a page with prices, an article — as the bottom. The reel brings the stranger. The page converts them, next week, when they finally decide.

## The part Instagram is genuinely bad at

Instagram is a broadcast tool that happens to have a messaging app attached. It is superb at putting your work in front of someone who has never heard of you.

It is poor at everything after that:

- Nobody can search your account for "brass lamp under 2000".
- Your past posts are effectively unfindable after about three weeks.
- Your prices live in captions, which you cannot bulk-edit.
- You cannot tell who asked about the blue one last month.
- If the account is restricted or lost, so is the shop.

None of that is a reason to leave. It is a reason not to let Instagram be the *only* place your business exists. The accounts that survive are the ones where Instagram is the front door and something you control is the room behind it.

### What to do this week

1. Rewrite the bio to say what you sell, where you ship from, and where prices live.
2. Pin one post with four items and their prices.
3. Put every current item somewhere with [a price you have worked out](/blog/how-to-price-handmade-products) beside it, and point the bio link there.
4. Add the price and the exact next step to every selling caption for two weeks.
5. Check profile-visits-to-messages, not followers.

The first four take an afternoon. The fifth changes what you work on next month.
