A return policy is not a concession you make to buyers. It is a document that protects you, because without one every disagreement is decided by whoever is more persistent, and that is rarely the person who made the item.

## Write it before you need it

The worst time to decide your policy is during the conversation that requires it. You will be annoyed, the buyer will be annoyed, and whatever you agree will become the precedent you have to honour next time.

A workable policy for a small seller fits in a short block on the page, and answers five things:

1. **How long** they have to raise a problem. Seven days from delivery is common and defensible.
2. **What qualifies.** Damaged in transit, wrong item, not as described.
3. **What does not.** Change of mind, custom or made-to-order pieces, sale items, normal variation in handmade work.
4. **Who pays return shipping**, and it should depend on whose fault it is.
5. **Refund or replacement**, which you choose, and how long it takes.

Publish it where the buyer will see it before they pay, not after. A policy that only appears in a dispute reads as invented on the spot.

> The policy's real audience is not the buyer who complains. It is you, at the moment you are deciding what is fair while upset.

## The three cases, and how each should go

**It arrived broken.** Your problem, without argument. Ask for a photograph of the item and the packaging — the packaging photograph is what you use with the courier, and you will want it. Then replace or refund quickly and without making them prove anything further. This case is cheap to handle well and enormously expensive to handle badly.

**It is not what they expected.** This is the case that hurts, because it is usually [a listing problem](/blog/how-to-write-a-product-description) rather than a product problem. If the colour, size, or finish was not clearly stated, take the return and fix the listing that afternoon. If it was clearly stated, with the measurement and the photograph, you are entitled to decline — politely, referring to the specific line.

**They changed their mind.** You are not obliged to accept this, and for made-to-order work you should not. Say so kindly and early. What you can offer instead: an exchange, or credit against a future order. Both keep the relationship without paying for it twice.

## The measurement that ends most disputes

Most "not as described" returns are actually size surprises. The single most effective preventive step in the whole of small-scale selling is a photograph of the item in a hand, plus the dimensions written in the description.

Do that and this category of return largely disappears. It is worth more than any amount of careful policy wording — see [product photos taken on a phone](/blog/product-photography-with-a-phone) for how to shoot the scale frame.

## Handling it in chat without losing the plot

When the conversation is in WhatsApp or DMs, three habits keep it from going badly:

- **Answer within a day, even if the answer is "I'm looking into it."** Silence converts a disappointed buyer into an angry one faster than a refusal does.
- **Ask for photographs first, always.** Not as suspicion — as procedure. "Could you send a photo of the item and one of the box it came in?" is neutral, standard, and it gives you what you need for the courier claim.
- **Summarise the resolution in one message.** *"Replacing the grey mug, dispatching Thursday, no charge — you can keep the damaged one."* This is the message you will be glad exists in three weeks.

## Do not confuse a policy with the law

A published policy governs the ordinary case. It does not override consumer protection law, which in India includes rules on unfair trade practices, misleading descriptions, and e-commerce obligations around seller identity and grievance redressal.

Two practical consequences. A policy that says "no returns under any circumstances" is not enforceable against a genuinely defective or misdescribed item, and stating it makes you look worse in the disagreement, not better. And if your sales cross into a scale where the e-commerce rules clearly apply to you, the obligations around disclosure and complaint handling are worth a conversation with someone qualified rather than a guess. This is an overview, not legal advice.

## Measure the rate, not the incidents

Individual returns feel personal. The number that matters is the percentage.

Track how many orders come back, and why, in one line each. After fifty orders the pattern is visible, and it is almost always concentrated: one item, one courier route, one photograph that oversells the colour. Fix that one thing and the rate halves.

A return rate near zero is not necessarily good news either. It sometimes means buyers who were disappointed did not bother to tell you and simply never came back — which costs more, and is invisible in the numbers you are looking at.

## The generous case

For an item under a certain value, replacing it without investigation is often cheaper than the conversation. Work out what that value is for your business.

Below it, say yes quickly. The refund costs you the item. The argument costs you the item, an hour, and a person who tells eleven others how it went.
