There is a particular experience of going home in your thirties and noticing that the brass vessel your mother kept the flour in is the exact object you have been seeing in expensive shops. Or that the steel dabbas, the cane basket, the stone mortar, the woven mat are all things you would now pay for.

This is not entirely nostalgia and it is not entirely fashion. Some of it is that those objects were genuinely better designed than what replaced them.

## What got thrown out, and why

Through the eighties and nineties, an enormous amount of Indian household equipment was replaced — brass and bronze by steel and then plastic, clay by aluminium, cane and cotton by nylon.

The reasons were real. Plastic is light, cheap, unbreakable and needs no maintenance. Steel does not need tinning. Aluminium heats fast. For households doing a great deal of work with limited time and money, these were improvements.

What was lost was less obvious at the time: material that ages well, objects that could be repaired, and things made locally by people you could go back to.

> The old things did not come back because they are old. They came back because the properties that made them expensive to maintain also made them last.

## The objects worth looking for

**The uruli and the brass kadhai.** Heavy, unlined pieces that were the workhorse of a kitchen. Most are now used for water and flowers rather than cooking, which is sensible — [unlined brass and acidic food do not mix](/blog/brass-bronze-copper-guide), and the tin lining needs redoing periodically.

**Kansa and bronze plates.** Bronze is less reactive than brass and was the traditional eating metal for exactly that reason.

**The stone mortar — ammikkal, sil batta, khalbatta.** Heavy, unbreakable, and genuinely better than a mixer for small quantities of wet spice, because it crushes rather than cuts.

**Clay pots.** The matka cools water through evaporation with no electricity, which is an elegant piece of physics. Clay handis change how a slow-cooked dish tastes.

**Cast iron.** Tawas and kadhais that improve with use and can be resurfaced indefinitely.

**Cane and bamboo.** Baskets, winnowing trays, mats. Light, repairable, and they age to a better colour.

**Copper vessels for water.** The traditional use, and the one copper is actually good at — plain water only, nothing acidic.

## The honest caveats

Not everything old was better, and there is a version of this nostalgia that ignores real problems.

**Weight is real.** A brass vessel a grandmother lifted daily is genuinely heavy, and the people who used them did not romanticise them.

**Maintenance is real.** Tinning needs redoing. Brass needs polishing or a decision to let it darken. Cast iron needs to stay dry. Clay needs seasoning before first use and will eventually crack.

**Not all of it is food-safe as sold.** Decorative brass is frequently lacquered; old vessels may have lost their lining. Ask, and look for copper colour showing through a tinned surface.

**Some of it is reproduction.** A great deal of "vintage" brass in decor shops is new, cast, thin and made to look old. Weight is the honest tell — old hand-raised pieces are heavy for their size and show hammer marks inside.

## How to tell what you are looking at

If you are digging through what is already in the family, four checks:

**Weight for size.** Hand-raised and cast pieces are heavy. Thin, light and perfectly uniform means spun, which is modern and cheap.

**Marks inside.** Hammer marks on the interior surface mean the piece was raised by hand from a sheet.

**The base.** Turned or lathe-finished bases with a small central mark; old pieces often show a slightly irregular foot.

**Repairs.** Old vessels frequently show a patch, a re-tinned interior, a replaced handle. These are not damage. They are evidence the object was worth mending, which is the entire argument for it.

## What to actually do with it

**Use it.** The single best outcome. An uruli with water and flowers by a door, a bronze plate you eat off on Sundays, a stone mortar for one spice paste a week.

**Have it restored rather than replaced.** Tinning is still done in most cities. Cane can be rewoven. Cast iron can be stripped and reseasoned. These services survive because the objects are worth the trouble.

**Do not over-polish.** A settled patina is protective and is what the piece is supposed to look like. Polishing weekly for decades measurably thins the metal.

**Ask where it came from.** Who made it, which town, whether it was bought or given. That is the part that is not recoverable once the generation that knows has gone, and it is what makes the object worth keeping rather than worth having.

## And if there is nothing in the family

All of it is still made. Not in every village and not by everyone, but the crafts are alive — [several of them more alive than most people realise](/blog/indian-crafts-that-deserve-a-comeback). Buying a new hand-raised brass vessel from someone who still makes them is not a lesser version of inheriting one. It is the same transaction your grandmother made.
