Advice about what people buy at different ages is usually a set of claims about personality — that one decade is adventurous and another has settled down. Most of it does not survive contact with actual people.

What genuinely changes is more boring and much more useful: your circumstances. How stable the income is. How permanent the housing is. How much storage exists. How many other people you are buying for. What you already own.

Those five change on a fairly predictable schedule, and they explain most of the difference in what makes sense to buy.

## The five things that actually shift

**Income stability, not income level.** Early on, the constraint is often unpredictability rather than amount — which argues for cheaper, replaceable things and against long commitments. Later, predictability makes it rational to buy once and well.

**Housing permanence.** Somebody who may move within a year should not buy furniture and should buy things that fit in a suitcase. Somebody settled can buy the heavy thing.

**Storage.** This constrains more purchases than money does, and it is the reason a large decorative object is a poor gift for a shared flat and a good one for a house.

**Who else you are buying for.** The number of birthdays, weddings and festivals you are responsible for rises steeply and then plateaus. This is usually the single biggest change in anyone's actual spending.

**What you already own.** The largest difference between a first purchase and a fifteenth is that the fifteenth has to go with fourteen things.

> Nothing here is about what people want at different ages. It is about what the circumstances make sensible, and the circumstances are not mysterious.

## Early on: buy range, cheaply

When you do not yet know what you reach for, the useful purchase is information.

**Buy several inexpensive things rather than one expensive one.** [Silver rather than gold is exactly this logic](/blog/gold-vs-silver-which-suits-you) — eight pieces for the price of one means eight experiments.

**Avoid anything that assumes permanence.** Heavy furniture, large decorative pieces, anything that has to be shipped.

**Buy the daily object well anyway.** The exception to buying cheap. The mug, the bag, the [one piece of jewellery that never comes off](/blog/everyday-jewellery-that-lasts) — those are used hundreds of times a year and the cheap version gets replaced four times.

**Notice what survives.** After a year, look at what is actually in rotation. That is your specification for everything afterwards.

## The middle: buy the proven thing, properly

Once you know what you reach for, the sensible move inverts.

**Buy the form you have proven, in a better material.** You have worn small hoops for six years — that is when a gold pair makes sense, not before. [The order matters and most people do it backwards.](/blog/your-first-gold-piece)

**Buy for the home, because the home is now stable.** This is when [brass, stoneware and handloom start to make sense](/blog/what-makes-a-house-feel-like-home) — they reward staying still.

**Buy fewer, better, in natural fibre.** The wardrobe stops being about range and starts being about coherence.

**Expect gift-buying to become the dominant category.** More occasions, more people, and a real need for a repeatable answer rather than a fresh decision each time.

## Later: buy for permanence and pass things on

**The purchases get heavier and less frequent.** Objects intended to outlast you, and to be given rather than replaced.

**Restoration starts beating replacement.** Tinning redone, cane rewoven, [old jewellery altered or remade rather than sold](/blog/restyling-inherited-jewellery). This is usually better value than buying new and it is nearly always more interesting.

**Provenance starts to matter more than newness.** Where a thing came from, who made it, who had it before. That is the part that cannot be bought later, and it is the difference between an object being kept and being cleared out.

## What does not change

Worth saying, because most age-segmented advice implies otherwise.

The checks are the same at every stage. [How to tell handloom from powerloom](/blog/handloom-vs-powerloom). Whether a metal is safe for food. Whether a hallmark is real. What "handmade" is allowed to mean. None of that is a beginner's concern that you graduate from — it is the whole skill, and it is as useful at fifty as at twenty-two.

The things that get kept are also the same at every stage: used, improving with age, plain enough not to date, repairable, with a story attached. [That list does not vary by decade.](/blog/gifts-she-will-actually-keep)

## The one genuinely age-related thing

The single biggest predictor of a good purchase is how much you already know about what you reach for — and that knowledge is bought with time rather than money.

Which means the honest advice for anyone at the start is not "buy this category." It is: buy cheaply, buy variously, pay attention to what you actually use, and let that decide what you buy expensively later.

Everyone who owns things they love did that, whether or not they noticed they were doing it.
