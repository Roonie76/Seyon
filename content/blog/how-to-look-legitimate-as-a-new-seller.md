A buyer who has never heard of you is not evaluating your product. They are evaluating the risk that you are not real.

This is not cynicism about Indian online buyers. It is a rational response to a market where anyone can make an account, post photographs they did not take, and disappear. Everything below is about making that risk visibly small in the thirty seconds a stranger gives you.

## The signals that get checked

In rough order of how often they decide it:

**A name and a place.** Not a handle. A person or a business name, and a city. "Made in Jaipur" is worth more than any amount of design, because it is falsifiable and specific.

**Photographs that are obviously yours.** Stock photography is the fastest way to lose a first-time buyer, and buyers detect it more reliably than sellers assume. Slightly imperfect photographs of the real item beat perfect photographs of something generic.

**Prices, visible, without asking.** A shop that hides prices reads as one that decides them per customer.

**Evidence that someone else has done this.** Reviews, a photograph from a buyer, a count of orders. One real review outweighs a great deal of self-description. [What buyers check before they message you](/blog/what-buyers-check-before-they-message-you) goes through this in more detail.

**A way to reach you that is not only a DM.** An email address, a phone number, a contact page. It suggests permanence.

**Something dated.** A post from four months ago, a "since 2024", an order history. Time is the hardest signal to fake and the most reassuring.

> Trust is not built by saying you are trustworthy. It is built by being checkable.

## What to do in your first month, with no reviews yet

The hardest position is genuine and new. Some things that work:

1. **Show the making.** Process photographs and video are the strongest available proof that a person and a workshop exist. This is why work-in-progress content converts better than finished-product content for new sellers.
2. **Show yourself once.** A single photograph or short video of you, with your name, in the place where you work. It is uncomfortable and it does more than a month of posts.
3. **Show the packing.** Orders being wrapped and labelled is evidence of a functioning business, not just a maker.
4. **Say what happens if it is wrong**, before anyone asks. A visible returns policy is a claim you are willing to be held to.
5. **Ask your first buyers for a photograph and a line.** Not a review request — a favour, personally worded. About half will say yes, and five pieces of proof from [ten orders](/blog/how-to-get-your-first-customers-online) is enough to stop looking new.

## Be specific about the things sellers are usually vague about

Vagueness is the tell. Each of these, stated plainly, moves someone from "maybe" to "fine":

- **Dispatch time.** "Ships within 3 working days", not "ships soon".
- **Delivery window.** "4–7 days across India", not "fast delivery".
- **Materials.** "Solid brass, unlacquered — it will develop a patina", not "premium metal".
- **Dimensions.** In centimetres, on every item.
- **Who makes it.** You, a workshop, a family unit, a co-operative. Say which.
- **Where it ships from.** A city name.

None of these are marketing. They are all just facts, and having them written down is itself the signal — it means you have thought about the buyer's experience before they arrived.

## The things that actively hurt

**"100% genuine, trusted seller, best quality."** Every seller claims this, including the ones who are not. It costs you credibility rather than building it.

**A brand new account with a thousand followers.** Buyers read the mismatch.

**Only accepting payment to a personal account with no name shown.** If this is your situation, say the name out loud in the chat before you send the details.

**Stock photos with a watermark from somewhere else.** Immediate disqualification.

**No response for three days.** Slow replies read as gone.

**Different prices in different places.** The buyer who finds both assumes the higher one was aimed at them.

## Being findable is itself a trust signal

The last one is the least obvious and matters more over time.

A buyer who searches your business name and finds a page, a listing, an article, a review — anything with a date on it — resolves the risk question immediately. A buyer who searches and finds nothing at all is left with only your word.

This is the quiet argument for existing somewhere public and indexable in addition to wherever you sell day to day. Not because search brings traffic, though it does eventually. Because a stranger who is checking on you needs something to find.

## The standard to hold

Everything above reduces to one question you can ask of your own shop: *if I were being careful with ₹1,500, would this be enough?*

Look at your profile as someone who has never heard of you. Name, place, price, proof, and a way to reach a human. If any one of the five is missing, that is the one costing you the buyers who never messaged.
