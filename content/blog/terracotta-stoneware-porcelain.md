Pottery is sold by look and used by physics. The single most useful thing to know is what temperature a piece was fired at, because that decides whether it can hold water, survive a flame, go in a dishwasher, or last a decade.

Three broad families, in ascending order of firing temperature.

## Terracotta and earthenware

Fired lowest. The clay does not vitrify — it stays porous, which is the whole character of the material.

**Porous means it breathes.** Water slowly seeps through the wall and evaporates, cooling what is inside. That is exactly why a *matka* keeps water cold with no electricity, and it is a genuinely elegant piece of physics.

It also means unglazed terracotta will always weep slightly, will stain, and will absorb odours. A glaze seals the surface but not the body, so a chip exposes porous clay again.

**Good for:** water pots, planters, serving, curd setting, decoration, tandoor-style cooking on a flame if the piece is made for it.
**Bad for:** freezer to oven, dishwashers, anything you need permanently watertight.

A new terracotta vessel should be seasoned — soaked in water for several hours, sometimes rubbed with oil — before first use. Skipping this is why people think their pot leaks.

## Stoneware

Fired hot enough that the clay body vitrifies. The material becomes non-porous in itself, whether or not it is glazed. Dense, heavy, opaque, tough.

This is what most good studio pottery is: mugs, plates, bowls, bakers. It holds heat, it survives daily use, and it chips rather than shattering.

**Good for:** everyday tableware, oven use if the maker says so, dishwasher and microwave in most cases.
**Bad for:** sudden temperature changes — freezer straight into a hot oven will crack it.

**Longpi**, the black pottery of Manipur, sits in this territory and is unusual: made without a wheel from weathered rock and serpentinite, and genuinely flame-safe. Its black is the material rather than a glaze.

## Porcelain

Fired highest, from a white clay body, and vitrified to the point of translucency. Hold a thin porcelain piece to a bright light and you will see the light come through.

Hard, non-porous, light for its strength, and the most refined of the three. Bone china is a porcelain variant with ash in the body, which makes it stronger and whiter and lets it be made thinner still.

**Good for:** fine tableware, anything that must not stain, dishwasher and microwave unless there is metallic decoration.
**Bad for:** direct flame, and gold or platinum rims never go in a microwave.

> If you remember one line: terracotta breathes, stoneware works, porcelain refines.

## Reading a piece in your hands

**Look at the unglazed foot ring.** Almost every piece has a bare patch of clay on the base. Reddish-brown and slightly rough means earthenware. Grey, brown or speckled and hard means stoneware. Bright white and smooth means porcelain.

**Tap it.** Earthenware gives a dull thud. Stoneware gives a low ring. Porcelain gives a clear, sustained ring.

**Hold it to the light.** Only porcelain glows through.

**Weigh it in your hand.** Stoneware is heavy for its size. Porcelain is light for its strength.

## Handmade versus production

Three things to look for on a wheel-thrown piece:

**Throwing rings** — faint spiral ridges inside a bowl or up the wall of a mug, left by the potter's fingers as the clay turned.

**A trimmed foot** — a slightly irregular chamfer where the base was pared back on the wheel, sometimes with a spiral of tool marks.

**Variation between pieces** — two mugs from the same maker differ in height by a few millimetres and in glaze by more than that.

A perfectly matched set with a seam line and identical glaze everywhere is slip-cast production. That is fine and much cheaper; it is simply not [what handmade prices are for](/blog/what-handmade-is-allowed-to-mean).

### On glaze safety

The question worth asking a small maker directly: **is the glaze food-safe, and is it lead-free?** A studio potter will have a straight answer. Bright, glossy low-fire glazes, particularly reds and oranges on imported decorative ware, are the historical source of lead concerns. Decorative pieces are not automatically dinnerware.

## Care

Season new terracotta before use. Let everything come to room temperature before it meets heat or cold. Wash pottery early rather than letting food dry in it.

Hairline crazing in a glaze — that fine web of lines — is usually cosmetic and normal, and it will stain over time in a way many people like. If it appears suddenly after a temperature shock, the piece has been stressed and it is time to demote it to holding pens.

Everything here breaks eventually. It is a material that is meant to be used, and a chipped mug you drank from every morning for six years was a better purchase than a perfect one in a cupboard.
