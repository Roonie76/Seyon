A first salary is a small amount of money with a disproportionate amount of meaning attached, and most of it goes on things nobody remembers. That is fine — some of it should. But it is one of the few moments where buying one object deliberately makes sense, because you will be able to point at it for thirty years.

The question is which object.

## What makes something last thirty years

Not price. Three properties, and they are the same ones that decide [what anybody keeps](/blog/gifts-she-will-actually-keep):

**It gets used.** Purely decorative things do not survive four house moves. Things that get picked up several times a week do.

**It improves rather than degrades.** Solid metal wears smooth. Cotton and silk soften. Brass darkens into patina. Wood takes a hand polish. Plating wears through, synthetics pill, and moulded resin just gets older.

**It can be repaired.** A clasp replaced, a vessel re-tinned, a seam restitched. Anything that cannot be mended is on a timer, however good it is.

> The expensive thing bought to mark the moment is frequently not the thing still owned. The plain thing bought to be used usually is.

## The categories that survive

**One piece of jewellery in a plain form.** Not a set. Small hoops, a plain band, one bangle, a short chain. Solid metal — [silver if the budget is small, gold if it stretches](/blog/gold-vs-silver-which-suits-you), never plated, because plating fails exactly where it shows. If you go gold, [there are six decisions worth getting right](/blog/your-first-gold-piece) and the hallmark is the one with legal weight behind it.

**A watch, if you wear one.** Mechanical watches are serviceable indefinitely, which is genuinely rare.

**One good vessel you use daily.** A hand-thrown mug, a bronze plate, a copper bottle. Absurdly small as a milestone purchase and consistently the thing people report still using.

**A tool for the thing you actually do.** A knife if you cook. A pen if you write by hand. An instrument. These get better with use in a way nothing decorative does.

**Something for the parents, if that is the impulse.** Very common and worth doing well rather than expensively — the object gets kept regardless, so it may as well be a good one.

## What tends not to survive

**Electronics.** Obsolete in five years, however much they cost. Buy them because you need them, not to mark anything.

**The aspirational garment.** Bought for a version of your life that does not exist yet. It hangs there as a reproach.

**Anything plated or "gold-toned."** The worst category in this whole list. Looks identical to solid metal for four months.

**A set of anything.** One element gets lost or broken and the rest become a burden.

**The largest thing the budget allows.** Size is not significance. The thing you can carry is the thing that survives the moves.

## The practical version

If you want a plan rather than a list:

1. **Spend a modest amount on something you will use every single day.** A mug, a pen, a piece of jewellery that stays on.
2. **Spend a little more on one thing made by a person, and find out who.** [Ask where it was made and by whom](/blog/buying-from-an-independent-maker) — that is what turns an object into something with a story attached, and the story is why it gets kept.
3. **Write the date somewhere.** Inside the box, on a slip of paper, in a note on your phone. Twenty years later this is the difference between "I've had this a while" and "I bought this the week I started working."

That third step costs nothing and it is the one everyone skips.

## On spending it on other people

A large share of first salaries in India go to family, and there is nothing to correct about that.

One small suggestion: if you are buying for a parent, buy something that gets used rather than displayed, and say plainly what it is and where it came from. [The sentence about the object is doing half the work of the gift](/blog/gifts-under-1000-that-dont-look-it), and it is the part people repeat when someone asks.

## The unglamorous conclusion

The best first-salary purchase is usually small, plain, made of something that ages well, and used constantly.

It is not the impressive thing. It is the thing that is still in daily use when you have forgotten what the salary was.
