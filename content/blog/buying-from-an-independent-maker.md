Ordering from someone who makes the thing themselves is a different transaction from ordering from a shop, and most of what goes wrong comes from treating it like the same one.

Nothing here is about being careful with strangers. It is about the fact that a workshop is not a warehouse.

## The thing to understand first

**Handmade means made.** Not picked from a shelf — made, after you order, by a person with a queue.

That single fact explains almost every friction point: why lead times are long, why "in stock" is sometimes optimistic, why a colour cannot always be repeated exactly, and why festival season is chaos. It also explains the advantages, which are real and mostly unused by buyers.

> A shop's job is to have it ready. A maker's job is to make it well. Those produce different timelines and you get to choose which one you want.

## What to ask before ordering

Four questions cover most of it, and asking them is normal rather than rude.

**1. Is this ready, or made to order?** The answer changes everything downstream. "Ready" means days; "made to order" means weeks.

**2. What is the realistic dispatch date?** Not delivery — dispatch. A maker controls when it leaves; the courier controls the rest. Ask for the date they will hand it over.

**3. What exactly am I getting?** Dimensions in centimetres, material, weight if it matters. Scale is the single most common disappointment in craft buying, and it is entirely avoidable by asking.

**4. What will it do over time?** Copper darkens. Lacquer scratches. Natural dye softens and bleeds for the first few washes. Handloom relaxes. All of that is fine when you expect it and feels like a fault when you do not.

## The advantages nobody uses

This is the part most buyers leave on the table. You are talking to the person who makes it, which means you can ask for things.

- **A different size.** Longer chain, shorter sleeve, deeper bowl.
- **A different colour** from the same range.
- **A change to the construction.** Heavier gauge wire, screw backs instead of push backs, a stronger clasp. [These decide whether a piece survives daily wear](/blog/everyday-jewellery-that-lasts) and they are rarely offered by default.
- **A second one to match**, now or later.
- **A photograph before dispatch**, especially for colour.

Most of these cost little or nothing. Almost nobody asks.

## Variation is the product

Two pieces will not be identical, and a maker who promises they will be is either using a mould or about to disappoint you.

What variation should look like: slight differences in height, a [block print repeat that steps](/blog/block-print-vs-screen-print), hammer marks inside a vessel, a glaze that pooled differently. What it should not look like: poor finish. Rough work is rough work, and "it's handmade" has become the standard excuse for it. [Genuine handwork is usually better finished than production in the places that matter.](/blog/what-handmade-is-allowed-to-mean)

## Ordering around festivals

The predictable failure. Between roughly September and November, and again in the wedding months, small makers are at capacity.

- **Order three to four weeks ahead**, not one.
- **Ask directly what they can promise**, and believe the answer rather than the hope.
- **Ask about gift packing before you order.** Many do it beautifully; some do not do it at all.
- **Ask for the invoice to be sent to you**, not put in the box.

## Paying, sensibly

Small makers frequently take payment by UPI or bank transfer rather than a card gateway, and for a made-to-order piece an advance is normal — the materials are bought for your order specifically.

Reasonable practice on both sides: an advance for made-to-order, balance before dispatch. Keep the conversation in writing, and get one message that summarises what was agreed — the item, the price, the address, the dispatch date. That single message prevents nearly every dispute, and it is as useful to the maker as it is to you.

## When something goes wrong

It sometimes will, and the good outcome depends mostly on how the first message is written.

**Photograph the item and the packaging.** The packaging photograph is what the maker needs for a courier claim, and asking for it is procedure rather than suspicion.

**Say what happened plainly**, without deciding fault in the first message.

**Give it a day.** Small makers are often making, not on their phone.

Most independent makers replace or refund quickly when something arrives broken, because their business runs on the next person's recommendation. The cases that go badly are almost always the ones where expectations were never written down.

## The short version

Ask what it is and when it ships. Ask for the changes you want — the answer is usually yes. Order earlier than feels necessary. Get the agreement in one message. Expect variation, and expect the object to change as it ages.

Do those and this is a better way to buy things than the alternative, which is the entire point.
