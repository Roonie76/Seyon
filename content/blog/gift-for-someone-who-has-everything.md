"They have everything" is almost never true. What it usually means is that the person is comfortable enough to buy anything they actively want, which is a different and more tractable problem.

If someone buys what they want, the gap is not in things they want. It is in three other places.

## The three real gaps

**Things they would enjoy but would never justify.** Most people who can afford anything still have a private rule about what is silly to spend money on. A very good version of something ordinary — tea, soap, a mug, a notebook — sits in that gap. They will not buy it and they will use it every day.

**Things they do not know exist.** The genuinely hard part of buying for someone with wide taste is that they have seen everything in the obvious categories. They have not seen [Bidri ware, or Longpi pottery, or palm-leaf etching](/blog/things-independent-makers-sell), because those are not sold anywhere they shop. Novelty is available; it is just not on the high street.

**Things that are about them specifically.** Not personalised in the engraved-name sense, which is often worse. Something that only makes sense because of who they are — a book on the thing they will not stop talking about, a piece from the place they lived, a tool for the hobby they have just started.

> Someone who has everything does not need another good thing. They need a thing they would not have found.

## What works, concretely

**Consumables of unreasonable quality.** The single most reliable answer. Real attar. Single-estate tea. Very good honey or spice. Handmade soap. These are used up, which means no shelf obligation, and at the top end they are genuinely better than what the person buys for themselves.

**Something from a craft they have not encountered.** This is where an unfamiliar tradition earns its place. A dhokra figure, a Longpi bowl, a piece of Bidri, an Etikoppaka object. The value is not that it is exotic — it is that it is unfamiliar to someone whose problem is familiarity.

**An upgrade of something they use constantly.** Look at what they replace often, or what they use in a cheap version daily. A tote, a lamp, a set of mugs, a shawl. This is the highest-hit-rate observation in gift-buying and it works at every budget.

**Something that takes time.** A made-to-order piece, something commissioned, something they have to wait for. The waiting is part of it, and it is the opposite of the instant availability that created the problem.

**An experience, if the person is a clearer rather than a keeper.** Some people find objects oppressive. For them, the right gift is something that leaves nothing behind.

## What reliably fails

**Another good version of a category they already have.** They have a nice scarf. A nicer scarf does not move.

**Anything they will feel obliged to display.** Large decorative objects commit someone else's wall.

**Gift cards**, unless you are certain the person prefers them. It reads as the transaction it is.

**Very expensive things bought to solve the problem with money.** Price does not fix the mismatch; it makes the mismatch more awkward.

**Personalised in the printed sense.** A name on a mug narrows an object to one use and one aesthetic.

## The observation that does most of the work

Ask yourself what the person complains about.

Not seriously — the small recurring irritations. The pen that runs out. The bag that is not quite the right size. The mug that does not keep tea hot. The lamp that is too bright to read by.

People rarely fix their own small irritations because each one is individually too trivial to act on. Fixing one for them is more precisely aimed than any expensive object, and it demonstrates the thing a gift is supposed to demonstrate — that you were paying attention.

## When you genuinely do not know them well

Fall back to the categories with no taste burden: consumables, and objects with clear craft value that carry their own interest.

A well-made [handmade object with a story attached](/blog/gifts-under-1000-that-dont-look-it) works for almost anyone, because you are not asserting anything about their taste — you are showing them something. Say where it is from and how it is made when you give it. That sentence is doing most of the work.

## The uncomfortable truth

For someone who buys what they want, the object matters less than the evidence of thought. A modest, specific, well-explained thing beats an expensive generic one — reliably, and by a large margin.

Which means the effort is not in the shopping. It is in the ten minutes beforehand spent thinking about the actual person rather than the occasion. [That is the whole of gift-buying, really](/blog/gifting-guide-india), but it is most obviously true here, where money has already been ruled out as the solution.
