Look at the oldest thing in your wardrobe that you still wear. Not the thing you keep for sentiment — the thing you actually put on. It is almost never the most expensive thing you have owned, and it is almost never the most fashionable.

Working out why is a more useful shopping guide than any trend forecast.

## What the survivors have in common

Go through several people's long-serving clothes and the same properties keep appearing.

**A natural fibre.** Cotton, linen, silk, wool. They age by softening; synthetics age by pilling, shining at the friction points, and holding smell. A cotton kurta at year eight is better than it was new. A polyester one at year three is finished.

**A colour that is not a decision.** Indigo, black, white, cream, grey, olive, rust. Not because they are safe but because they do not date — a strong seasonal colour is legible as a specific year forever afterwards.

**A shape with room in it.** Anything cut close to the body has to keep fitting the same body, and bodies do not cooperate. Clothes that survive have some ease.

**Construction you can see.** Flat-felled or French seams rather than a raw edge zigzagged over. A hem deep enough to let down. Buttons sewn with a shank. These are visible in thirty seconds and they predict the next ten years.

**No hardware doing structural work.** Zips, clasps and elastic fail long before cloth does, and are the most common reason a good garment is thrown away.

> The clothes that last are rarely the ones bought as investments. They are the ones bought slightly plainer than the buyer wanted at the time.

## The five-second checks in a shop

**Pull the seam.** Hold the fabric either side of a seam and pull gently apart. You should not see the stitches gap and the fabric between them stretch open.

**Look at the inside.** Turn the garment inside out. Neat, enclosed seams and a proper facing at the neck mean somebody spent time. Overlocked raw edges everywhere means speed.

**Check the hem depth.** A hem of two or three centimetres can be let down or taken up. A rolled quarter-centimetre hem cannot be altered at all.

**Crush a corner and let go.** Natural fibres crease and recover softly. A fabric that springs back like new has synthetic content, whatever the label implies.

**Look at the buttons.** Are there spares? Are they sewn with a small stem of thread underneath rather than flat against the cloth? Both signal a maker who expected the garment to be worn for years.

## Where handmade genuinely wins, and where it does not

Handloom cotton and silk get better with washing in a way mill cloth does not — the weave relaxes and the hand softens. Ten-year-old handloom is the argument for handloom, and it takes ten years to make.

[Block-printed cloth](/blog/block-print-vs-screen-print) fades, and fades well, into soft chalky tones people pay to imitate. That is ageing, not damage — worth knowing before you are disappointed by it at year two.

Where handmade does not automatically win: fit and finish on tailored garments. A well-made industrial shirt has more consistent seams than most small-batch production. Handwork buys you material and character, not precision.

## What to stop buying

**Anything with visible branding.** It ties the garment to a year.

**Anything you would only wear with one other thing.** The other thing will wear out first.

**Anything that needs dry cleaning that you will not do.** Be honest. A garment you cannot maintain is a garment with a two-year life.

**The very cheap version of something you love.** You will buy it four times. The maths almost always favours buying it once, better.

**Anything bought to fit a body you intend to have.** It will hang there as a reproach and then be given away.

## The maintenance that actually extends life

Most clothes die of washing, not wearing.

- **Wash less, air more.** Most things worn for a few hours need airing, not a machine cycle.
- **Cold water, inside out, and a full load washed gently rather than a small load washed hard.**
- **Dry in shade.** Sun bleaches, and it is brutal on natural dye.
- **Repair early.** A seam caught at three stitches is a two-minute fix; the same seam at thirty is a garment lost.
- **Store folded, not hung**, for anything knitted or heavy — hangers stretch shoulders permanently.

## The buying rule that follows from all this

Buy fewer things, slightly plainer than you want, in natural fibre, from someone who will tell you how it was made.

Then wear them badly — that is the point. The clothes worth owning are the ones that improve under use, and a thing kept pristine in a cupboard has been wasted just as thoroughly as a thing worn out in a season.
