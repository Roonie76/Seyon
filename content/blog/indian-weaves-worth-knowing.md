Naming a weave is not connoisseurship. It is the practical difference between buying something that suits the weather you live in and buying something that will sit in a cupboard because it is too heavy to wear.

Six worth being able to recognise, and what each is actually for.

## Chanderi

From Chanderi in Madhya Pradesh. The characteristic quality is a transparency that no other Indian weave quite matches — the yarn is not degummed, which leaves the silk with its natural shine and gives the cloth a glassy, luminous body.

Usually woven as silk-cotton, sometimes pure silk, with fine zari motifs. Very light, holds a crisp fall rather than a fluid one.

**For:** heat, long days, occasions where you do not want weight. Not for cold.

## Maheshwari

From Maheshwar, also in Madhya Pradesh, on the Narmada. Traditionally a reversible border — the same border reads on both faces — and often stripes or fine checks in the body.

Lighter and more matte than Chanderi, with a distinctive flat drape. A Maheshwari is one of the easiest handloom saris to wear if you are not used to draping.

**For:** everyday, work, summer. The forgiving one.

## Kota Doria

From Kota in Rajasthan. Instantly identifiable by *khat* — the tiny square check formed by alternating thick and thin threads in both directions, visible against the light like graph paper.

Extraordinarily light. Cotton, silk, or a mix. It is close to weightless and almost transparent.

**For:** the hottest part of the year, and for people who find handloom too heavy.

## Jamdani

From Bengal, historically Dhaka. The technique is what matters: the motifs are not printed or embroidered but woven in by hand as the cloth is made, with extra weft thread inserted by the weaver against the ground weave.

This means the design has no reverse side in the usual sense, and it means the work is slow — a fine Jamdani can take months. The motifs seem to float in the cloth.

**For:** the piece you keep. It is not an everyday weave and it is not priced like one.

## Ikat

Not a place — a resist-dyeing method used across Odisha, Telangana, Gujarat and elsewhere. The yarn is tied and dyed *before* weaving, so the pattern exists in the thread and appears only as the cloth is woven.

The tell is the feathered edge on every motif — a slight blur where the dye met the tie. That softness is intrinsic. A crisp, hard-edged "ikat" is a print of one.

Single ikat resists only warp or only weft. **Double ikat**, of which Patan Patola in Gujarat is the famous example, resists both and requires the two to align thread by thread on the loom. It is among the most difficult textiles made anywhere.

**For:** anything, depending on weight. Ikat spans cotton daily wear to heirloom silk.

## Kanjeevaram

From Kanchipuram in Tamil Nadu. Heavy mulberry silk with substantial zari, and a structural signature: the body and the border are frequently woven separately and joined, so a strong contrast border can be attached to the body with a join you can see and feel.

Weight is the point. A Kanjeevaram has presence and it is not a hot-afternoon garment.

**For:** weddings, ceremony, the wardrobe you inherit and pass on.

> Weight and weather decide more of your actual wearing than beauty does. A sari too heavy for where you live is a beautiful object you will not use.

## A rough guide by climate

- **Very hot, humid:** Kota Doria, cotton Jamdani, fine Chanderi cotton-silk.
- **Hot, dry:** Maheshwari, Chanderi, cotton ikat.
- **Cool or air-conditioned:** silk ikat, silk Chanderi, Jamdani.
- **Cold, or ceremony regardless:** Kanjeevaram, heavy Patola, Banarasi.

## Two things to check whatever you buy

**The reverse.** In a handwoven piece with woven motifs, the back should show the working — cut threads, the structure of how the design was made. A design that is invisible on the back was probably printed on, which [these checks](/blog/handloom-vs-powerloom) will settle.

**The zari.** Real zari is metal thread; testing it is a specialist matter, but the honest question is whether it is pure, tested, or imitation, and any serious seller will answer. Imitation zari is not a scandal — it is most of the market — but it should be priced as what it is.

## Storing them

Fold along different lines each time. Silk breaks permanently along a crease that never moves, and the classic way to lose a Kanjeevaram is to leave it folded the same way for a decade.

Cotton or muslin bags, never plastic. Air them once or twice a year. Keep them away from naphthalene resting directly against the cloth.

They will outlast you if you let them.
