Most independent sellers price by looking at what someone else charges and going slightly under. It feels safe. It is the single most reliable way to work very hard for a living wage you would have refused if anyone had offered it to you as a salary.

The problem is not that the number is too low. It is that the number is a guess, so you cannot tell whether it is too low.

## The three numbers you need first

**Materials, per unit, all of them.** Not just the obvious ones. The thread, the polish, the box, the tissue, the tape, the label, the card you write on. Buy in whatever quantity you actually buy in, divide by the number of units it makes, and write the number down. Sellers routinely undercount this by thirty percent because the small things are bought in bulk and feel free.

**Your time, per unit, at a rate you chose.** Time the whole thing once, honestly — including the setup and the cleanup, not just the making. Then pick an hourly rate. Not the rate you think you deserve after you are established. The rate you need now. If you cannot name one, use what you would have to pay someone else to do this job properly.

**Overheads, per month, divided by units.** Rent or the share of the room, electricity, internet, tools wearing out, the phone, subscriptions, packaging you buy in advance. Add it up monthly, divide by a realistic number of units per month. Not your best month. An average one.

> If you have never written these three numbers down, you do not have a pricing problem yet. You have an arithmetic problem, and it is hiding the pricing problem.

## The base cost, and why it is not the price

Add the three together. That is what one unit costs you to exist. Call it the base cost.

The price is not the base cost plus a margin you feel good about. The price has to also absorb things that are invisible per-unit but real per-month:

1. **Payment and platform costs**, if any apply to how you sell.
2. **Shipping**, if you absorb it — and if you say "free shipping", you absorb it. [Working out what shipping actually costs you](/blog/shipping-for-small-sellers-india) comes before you can price it in.
3. **Returns, breakage, and remakes.** A percentage of what you send comes back or arrives wrong. If you have never measured it, assume it is not zero.
4. **The units you make and never sell.** Samples, mistakes, the colour nobody wanted.
5. **Discounts you have not planned yet.** Festival offers, the friend price, the bulk order.

A common working approach for handmade goods is to take the base cost, add the per-unit share of the above, and then apply a multiple — often somewhere between two and three times — to arrive at retail. The multiple is not greed. It is what lets you survive a slow month, a damaged shipment, and eventually pay someone else to help.

If that number frightens you, that is information. It usually means either the process takes too long for the item, or the item is priced into a market it does not belong in.

## Two mistakes that cost the most

**Pricing against a factory.** If your competitor's item is machine-made in ten thousand units, you cannot win on price and should not try. You are selling a different thing to a different person. Competing on price against manufacturing is how good makers quit.

**Pricing so low it reads as a warning.** Buyers use price as a signal about quality and about whether you will still exist in three months. A hand-thrown ceramic mug at ₹180 does not read as a bargain. It reads as a mistake, or as something that is not what it claims to be.

### Where to check yourself

Look at three sellers making genuinely comparable work — comparable materials, comparable time, comparable finish — and see where you land. If you are forty percent below all of them, you are not undercutting the market. You are subsidising it.

## Raising prices when you are already too low

Almost every seller reading this is under-priced and dreading the correction. Some things that make it survivable:

- **Raise on new items first.** New work has no anchor. Introduce the higher price there and let it become normal before you touch the existing range.
- **Change something visible at the same time.** Better packaging, a card, a small improvement in finish. The price is easier to accept when something is different.
- **Announce it once, without apology.** "Prices go up on the 1st — current prices hold until then." You will get a burst of orders and almost no complaints.
- **Expect to lose some buyers.** You will. Count what you keep. Twenty orders at ₹1,400 beats thirty at ₹850, and it is nine fewer things to make.

> The buyers you lose to a price rise were rarely the ones who came back anyway.

## Put the number where people can see it

Whatever you land on, the price belongs on the page, next to the item, before anyone has to ask.

"DM for price" costs you the buyers who did not want to negotiate, which is most of them, and it costs you the ones who assumed it was more than it is. It saves you nothing — you type the number anyway, forty times a week, to people who mostly do not buy. It is also [one of the first things a cautious buyer reads as a warning](/blog/what-buyers-check-before-they-message-you).

A price you are confident in is a price you can publish. If you cannot publish it, the work above is not finished.
