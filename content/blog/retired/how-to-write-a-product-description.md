Most product descriptions are written by someone who has held the item, for someone who has held the item. That is the whole problem. The reader has not held it, cannot ask, and is trying to decide whether to send money to a stranger.

A good description does exactly one thing: it removes the reasons not to buy, in the order the buyer thinks of them.

## What the buyer is actually asking

In rough order, before they will pay:

1. What is it, in plain words?
2. How big is it?
3. What is it made of?
4. What will it look like in my life?
5. How do I look after it?
6. When will it arrive?
7. What happens if it is wrong?

Nearly every description online answers the first and skips the rest. The skipped ones are what turns into a message, and a message is a step where people leave.

## The structure that works

**One sentence saying what it is.** Plain, unpoetic, complete. "A hand-thrown stoneware mug, 300ml, glazed in matte grey." Someone skimming should be able to stop here and know.

**Two or three sentences on the thing that makes it worth the price.** This is where the making goes — the material, the process, the reason it costs what it costs. Not adjectives. Facts that imply the adjectives. *"Thrown on the wheel and fired twice, so the glaze goes right to the rim"* does more than *"exquisite artisanal craftsmanship."*

**The specifics, as a list.** Dimensions in centimetres. Weight if it matters. Material. Colour, described honestly. What is included. This is the part people scroll to, and the part that [prevents returns](/blog/returns-and-refunds-small-business).

**Care, in one line.** Dishwasher or not. Hand wash or machine. Store flat or hanging. Say it even when it is obvious.

**What happens next.** Made to order or in stock, dispatch time, delivery estimate. A buyer who does not know when it arrives assumes the worst.

> Every unanswered question is a message you have to answer by hand, or a sale that ends in silence. Usually the second.

## Write the flaw down

The instinct is to hide variation. Do the opposite, and be specific about it:

*"Each piece is thrown by hand, so no two are the same height — expect a variation of up to half a centimetre. The glaze pools slightly at the base; this is intentional and part of the finish."*

This does three things. It sets the expectation, so the item cannot disappoint. It proves a person made it, which is the entire premise of buying handmade. And it reads as confidence, because only someone certain of their work describes its irregularities in writing.

Hidden variation becomes a return. Declared variation becomes a feature.

## Words that are costing you money

**"Beautiful", "stunning", "gorgeous", "premium", "high quality".** These are claims the buyer discounts automatically, because every seller makes them. They take up the space where a fact should be.

**"DM for price".** The most expensive four words in social commerce. Most people will not. [Work the number out](/blog/how-to-price-handmade-products) and publish it.

**"One size".** Give the measurement.

**"Perfect for gifting".** True of nearly everything, so it says nothing.

**Copied manufacturer text.** If you resell, rewrite it. Identical text on forty sites ranks nowhere and reads as a drop-shipper.

### Write the way someone searches

Somewhere in the description, use the words a stranger would type. Not stuffed — placed naturally, once or twice.

Someone does not search "artisanal drinkware vessel". They search "handmade ceramic mug", "brass table lamp", "cotton saree with zari border". Use their words in the first sentence, and again where they fit. This is most of what small-shop search optimisation amounts to, and it costs nothing.

## Length

Long enough to answer the seven questions. Usually 80 to 150 words plus a specifications list.

Shorter than that and you are leaving questions for the inbox. Much longer and nobody reads to the part that would have sold it. If you find yourself writing three paragraphs of atmosphere, cut them and put the dimensions there instead.

## A quick test before you publish

Read your description and ask: could I write this same paragraph about a different product?

If yes, it is not a description. It is decoration. Find the fact that is only true of this item — the width, the material, the number of hours, the place it was made — and lead with it.

Then read it once more as someone who has ₹1,400 and a small, reasonable fear of being disappointed. Everything that fear would ask, answer.
