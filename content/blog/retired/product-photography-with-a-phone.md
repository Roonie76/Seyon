The phone is not the limitation. Nearly every product photograph that fails online fails on light, background, or scale — and all three are free to fix.

What follows assumes you have a phone from the last few years, a window, and about an hour.

## Light: one window, no flash, no ceiling light

Turn off every light in the room. Open one curtain. Put the item on a surface beside the window, not facing it — light coming from the side reveals texture, light coming from behind the camera flattens it.

Overcast days are better than bright ones. Direct sunlight produces hard edges and blown-out highlights that no amount of editing recovers. If the sun is strong, hang a white bedsheet or a sheet of tracing paper over the window; the light becomes soft and even, which is what a studio softbox does for far more money.

Never use the flash. It produces a hard shadow, a colour cast, and a photograph that looks like evidence.

### The one accessory worth having

A piece of white foam board, or a sheet of white card, or a white cloth over a book — placed on the shadow side, opposite the window, an arm's length from the item. It bounces light back and lifts the dark side. It costs almost nothing and it is the difference between a photograph that looks amateur and one that does not.

> Two-thirds of the improvement available to you is one window, one white card, and the ceiling light switched off.

## Background: choose boring on purpose

Backgrounds should be plain, non-reflective, and a different tone from the product. Dark item, light background. Light item, mid-grey. A sheet of matte chart paper curved up the wall behind the item removes the horizon line and reads as professional immediately.

What to avoid: patterned cloth, wood grain with heavy texture under small items, anything shiny, and anything with a competing brand in frame.

Pick one background and use it for every product. A shop where the catalogue photographs sit on eleven different surfaces looks disorganised even when every individual photo is fine. Consistency is doing more work here than quality.

## Scale: the most-skipped, most-important shot

The most common complaint in returns and disputes is not quality. It is *"I thought it was bigger."*

Every item needs one photograph that establishes size beyond argument. In order of usefulness:

1. **In a hand.** Instantly readable, no explanation needed.
2. **In use, in a real place.** The bowl on a table with food in it. The earring worn.
3. **Beside a common object.** A coin, a phone, a standard cup.
4. **Dimensions written on the image.** Least appealing, most precise. Use it in addition, not instead — and [in the description](/blog/how-to-write-a-product-description) as well.

## The set to shoot for every item

Six photographs, in this order. It takes about ten minutes per item once you have the setup fixed.

1. **The hero.** Straight on, whole item, clean background, well lit. This is the one that appears everywhere.
2. **Scale.** In a hand or in use.
3. **Detail.** Close enough to see the material and the finish — the weave, the grain, the join.
4. **The other side.** The back, the underside, the clasp, the base.
5. **In context.** The item where it lives, styled lightly.
6. **The honest one.** The seam, the variation, the mark that comes from being made by a person. Include it deliberately. It prevents the disappointment that produces returns, and it reads as confidence.

### Settings, briefly

Wipe the lens first — this fixes more bad photographs than any other single action. Use the main camera, not the ultra-wide, which distorts edges. Step back and stay a little further away than feels natural; phone lenses exaggerate whatever is closest. Tap to focus on the item, then drag the exposure slider down slightly. Slightly under-exposed recovers; blown-out white does not.

If your phone has a grid option, turn it on and use it to keep the item level.

## Editing: less than you think

Three adjustments, in this order: straighten, crop, then raise the exposure a little if needed. Nothing else is necessary.

What to avoid entirely:

- **Filters.** They shift colour, and colour is the thing buyers will hold you to.
- **Heavy saturation.** A blue that arrives less blue than the photograph is a refund.
- **Aggressive background removal.** Cut-outs with rough edges look worse than a clean real background.

The standard to hold yourself to is not "beautiful". It is *"the buyer will not be surprised when they open the box."* Every editing decision that moves away from that is borrowing against [a return](/blog/returns-and-refunds-small-business).

## Consistency beats quality

If you shoot each item on the same background, from the same angles, at roughly the same distance, in the same light, your listings will look like a shop.

Set it up once — mark where the paper goes, where the item sits, where you stand — and photograph every new item the same way. Ten minutes each, forever, and the whole catalogue holds together.

That coherence is worth more to a stranger deciding whether you are real than any single spectacular photograph.
