Buying a sari is one of the few purchases where the vocabulary is genuinely intimidating and the price range genuinely enormous. The same six metres can cost eight hundred rupees or eighty thousand, and from across a shop the difference is not obvious.

What follows is the order to think in, which is not the order shops present things in.

## Start with the occasion, then the weather, then the weave

Almost everyone starts with "which weave" and gets lost, because there are dozens and each has a story. Start two steps earlier.

**What is it for?** Daily wear, work, a festival, a wedding you are attending, a wedding you are in. These want completely different weights of cloth.

**What is the weather where you will wear it?** This eliminates more options than anything else. A heavy Kanjeevaram in Chennai in May is a beautiful object you will not put on. [Weight and climate decide more of your actual wearing than beauty does.](/blog/indian-weaves-worth-knowing)

**Then the weave.** By this point the field has narrowed from thirty to about four.

## The five things to check with it in your hands

**1. The weight.** Hold the folded sari. This tells you more in three seconds than any label. Heavy means ceremony; light means you will actually wear it.

**2. The fall.** Ask them to open it and let a metre hang. Watch whether it falls in soft folds or stands away from itself. Stiff cloth holds pleats sharply and is harder to drape; fluid cloth drapes easily and creases more.

**3. The border join.** In many South Indian saris the border is woven separately and attached. Run your fingers along it — a proper join is neat and secure. This is normal construction, not a defect, but a badly finished one will fail.

**4. The blouse piece.** Check it is attached, check the length, and check the colour actually matches in daylight. Shop lighting is warm and a great many blouse pieces are a slightly different shade from the sari.

**5. Handloom or powerloom.** Ask directly. The price difference is several times over and the visual difference is small. [Six checks settle it in your hands](/blog/handloom-vs-powerloom) — the selvedge is the fastest one.

> Ask the price of the sari and the price of the blouse stitching separately, before you decide. Stitching, fall and pico add up to real money and are quoted last.

## What the price is actually made of

Four things, in rough order of impact:

**Fibre.** Pure silk, cotton, a blend, or polyester. A great deal of inexpensive "silk" is polyester, and it announces itself: it springs back too readily when crushed in your fist, and it feels slightly cool and slick rather than warm.

**Zari.** The metallic thread. Real zari uses silver wire, sometimes gilded; imitation zari is coated polyester. Imitation is most of the market and there is nothing wrong with it — it should simply be priced as what it is. Ask whether it is pure, tested, or imitation, and expect a straight answer.

**Handwork.** Handloom over powerloom, woven motifs over printed ones, and any hand embroidery on top.

**The name.** A famous weave carries a premium, and a GI tag tells you a piece genuinely comes from where it claims — nothing about whether it was handwoven or well made.

## The questions worth asking out loud

1. **Pure silk, or blend, or polyester?**
2. **Handloom or powerloom?**
3. **Real zari or imitation?**
4. **Where was it woven, and by whom?**
5. **How do I wash it?**

The fifth is the one people skip and regret. Some saris are dry clean only; many handloom cottons are better washed at home; naturally dyed pieces will release colour for the first several washes and need to be washed alone.

## Buying online, and the two things that go wrong

**Colour.** Screens are unreliable and shop photography is worse. Ask for a photograph taken in daylight, without a flash, next to something white for reference. Any serious seller will send one.

**Length and blouse.** Standard is around 5.5 metres, or 6.3 with the blouse piece. Confirm which you are getting. Also confirm whether the blouse piece is attached or separate.

## Two practical things nobody mentions

**Budget for the finishing.** Blouse stitching, fall attaching and pico edging can add several hundred to a couple of thousand rupees, and a sari without them is not wearable. Factor it in before you decide what you can afford.

**Buy the first one lighter than you want to.** Almost everyone's first serious sari is too heavy, because heavy looks impressive on a shelf. The one you will wear thirty times is the one you barely notice putting on.

## Storing it

Fold along different lines each time — silk breaks permanently along a crease that never moves. Cotton or muslin bags, never plastic. Air them once or twice a year, and keep naphthalene from resting directly against the cloth.

A good sari outlives the person who bought it. Most of the ones that do not are lost to a decade of being folded the same way.
