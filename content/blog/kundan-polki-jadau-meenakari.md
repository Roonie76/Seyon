Four words appear on almost every listing for traditional Indian jewellery, and they are used so loosely that two pieces described identically can differ in price by a factor of twenty. They are not synonyms. Three of them describe how a piece is made, and one describes what is set into it.

Knowing which is which is the difference between paying for a technique and paying for a word.

## The one that is a stone, not a method

**Polki** is uncut diamond. Not lab-grown, not an imitation — a real diamond in its natural state, cleaved and polished only on the surface that shows, with the back left flat and unfaceted.

That is why polki does not sparkle the way a brilliant-cut diamond does. It glows. Light does not bounce around inside it and shoot back at you; it sits in the stone. Under a foil backing, which is traditional, it takes on a warm, slightly milky quality that people either love immediately or find disappointing after modern diamonds.

Polki is expensive because it is diamond. Anything sold as polki at a price that seems impossible is almost certainly glass, quartz, or cubic zirconia, and the honest listings say so.

## The three that are methods

**Kundan** is a setting technique. Highly refined, almost pure gold is worked at room temperature into thin foil, and that foil is used to hold the stone in place — packed around it, burnished until it grips. No claws, no prongs, no adhesive doing the structural work.

This is why kundan pieces have that characteristic look of a stone sitting in a smooth, bright collar of gold. The technique is old, slow and skilled, and the reason a kundan setting can hold an irregular uncut stone that no prong setting would grip.

**Jadau** is the broader craft that kundan setting belongs to. A jadau piece is built up by embedding stones into a gold framework using the kundan method, often over a lac core that supports the structure from inside. Several artisans typically touch one piece: one shapes the frame, one sets, one engraves, one enamels.

In practice, "jadau" describes the whole object and "kundan" describes how the stones were held. You will see them used interchangeably. They are not quite.

**Meenakari** is enamel. Coloured glass powder fused onto metal at high heat, one colour at a time, in decreasing order of the temperature each colour needs. Reds are hardest and are laid first in the traditional Jaipur order.

The thing worth knowing about meenakari is where it lives: **on the back.** A properly made jadau necklace is enamelled on the reverse, the side nobody sees when it is worn. It was never for the viewer. Turn any piece over before you buy it — the back tells you far more about who made it than the front does.

> Front for the buyer, back for the maker. Judge a traditional piece by the side it was not trying to impress you with.

## What this means at the counter

Some combinations that are all legitimate and all differently priced:

- **Jadau with polki, enamelled reverse.** The full traditional object. Expensive, and should be.
- **Kundan setting with semi-precious or glass stones.** Real technique, modest materials. Often beautiful, often the sensible buy.
- **Kundan-*style*.** Stones glued or claw-set into a gold-coloured base, made to resemble the look. Nothing wrong with it as costume jewellery; a great deal wrong with it at kundan prices.
- **Meenakari on silver.** A common, lovely and much more affordable version of the enamel work — [how to buy silver properly](/blog/how-to-buy-silver-jewellery-india) applies.

### Four questions that settle it

1. **Is the stone diamond?** If polki is claimed, ask directly, in writing.
2. **How are the stones held?** Kundan foil, prongs, or adhesive.
3. **What is the base metal, and what purity?** Gold, silver, or a gold-plated alloy.
4. **Is there enamel on the reverse?** And can you see a photograph of it.

Any seller doing this properly will answer all four without hesitation, because the answers are what they are proud of.

## On hallmarks

If the piece is gold, hallmarking is the part with legal weight behind it. A hallmarked gold item in India carries three marks — the BIS triangle, the purity and fineness, and a six-character HUID you can check yourself in the BIS Care app.

Note what that does *not* cover. The hallmark certifies the gold. It says nothing about whether the stones are diamond, whether the setting is genuine kundan work, or whether the enamel is real meenakari or paint. Those remain matters of what the seller tells you, which is why the four questions above matter.

## The unromantic summary

Polki is a stone. Kundan is how it is held. Jadau is the whole made object. Meenakari is the enamel, and the good stuff is where you were not going to look.
