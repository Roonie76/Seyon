Most gift-buying goes wrong in the same place: at the point where you stop thinking about the person and start thinking about the occasion. The occasion tells you the budget and the deadline. It tells you almost nothing about what to buy.

What follows is a way of choosing that produces fewer polite thank-yous and more things people actually use.

## Start with how they live, not what they like

"She likes blue" is not enough to buy on. These questions are:

**Do they keep things or clear things?** Some people find objects comforting and some find them oppressive. For a clearer, the right gift is consumable — good tea, ittar, soap, something edible — not another item on a shelf.

**Do they entertain?** Serving pieces are a wonderful gift for someone who has people over and a burden for someone who does not.

**How much space do they have?** A large brass uruli is a beautiful thing in a house with floor space and a problem in a one-bedroom flat.

**Do they already have a strong style?** If yes, buy within it rather than trying to expand it. A gift is a poor vehicle for suggesting someone's taste should change.

**What do they replace often?** The thing someone buys repeatedly in a cheap version is often the thing they would love in a good version — a tote, a lamp, a set of mugs, a scarf.

> The best-received gifts are almost always an upgrade of something the person already uses, not an introduction to something new.

## What tends to land, by relationship

**Someone you know well.** Buy specifically. This is where a single beautiful object works — a hand-thrown mug in their colour, a piece of jewellery in a form you have seen them wear.

**Someone you know a little.** Consumables and neutral-good objects. Tea, ittar, handmade soap, a kantha throw, a set of two mugs, a copper bottle. Low risk of being wrong, no obligation to display.

**A household, or a wedding.** Serving and table pieces. Bronze or brass serveware, a set of bowls, table linen. Buy things that come in pairs or sets, and give the set.

**A colleague or a formal gift.** Objects with clear craft value and no personal taste burden — a Channapatna piece, a block-printed table runner, handmade paper stationery, a small dhokra figure.

**A child.** [Channapatna toys](/blog/indian-lacquerware-channapatna) are the obvious answer and remain a good one: turned wood, vegetable-dyed, safe to chew, and they survive being thrown.

**Yourself.** Buy the thing you have looked at three times. You already made the decision.

## Six categories that reliably work

1. **A pair of mugs or bowls.** Used daily, forgiving of taste, and immediately obvious that they were chosen rather than picked up.
2. **A kantha or handwoven throw.** Universally useful, no sizing problem, and better with age.
3. **Ittar or handmade soap.** Consumable, luxurious, small.
4. **A copper bottle.** Practical, used daily, and reads as a considered gift rather than an expensive one.
5. **A block-printed table runner or bedcover.** Flat, easy to post, easy to store if it is not their thing.
6. **[Silver](/blog/how-to-buy-silver-jewellery-india) in a plain form.** A simple oxidised piece is safer than a statement one, and easier to wear with anything.

## What to avoid unless you are certain

**Anything sized.** Rings, blouses, footwear. The return conversation ruins it.

**Strong fragrance for the home.** Incense and candles are intensely personal and frequently unwelcome.

**Religious or ritual objects**, unless you know exactly what is appropriate for that household. This goes wrong more often than any other category.

**Anything requiring maintenance you have not warned them about.** [Unlacquered brass](/blog/brass-bronze-copper-guide) that needs polishing, [silk](/blog/indian-weaves-worth-knowing) that needs particular washing. Give the care instructions with it, or buy something else.

**Very large decorative pieces.** They commit someone else's wall or floor.

## The practical part people skip

**Order earlier than feels necessary.** Handmade means made, and made-to-order genuinely takes time. For festival periods, allow two to three weeks and ask the maker directly what they can promise.

**Ask about gift packing before you order**, not after. Many small makers do it beautifully and some do not do it at all.

**Ask for the invoice to be sent to you**, not into the box.

**Write the card.** For a handmade object, a line about why you chose it is doing half the work of the gift. "This is made by a family in Bidar and the black is soil, not paint" turns an object into something with a story attached.

## The thing worth remembering

A gift is a statement about how well you were paying attention. Almost nobody minds an inexpensive object chosen precisely; a great many people are quietly unmoved by an expensive one chosen generically.

Attention is the budget that matters. The rest is logistics.
