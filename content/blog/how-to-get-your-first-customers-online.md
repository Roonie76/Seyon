Nobody's first ten customers come from an algorithm. They come from people who already had a reason to trust you, and from a very small number of strangers who were looking for exactly the thing you make on the day you happened to be visible.

Understanding that changes what you do in the first month, and stops you from spending it on the wrong work.

## Before anything else: be findable when someone tries

The first ten buyers will nearly all check you before they pay. Losing them at that step is the most common way a promising start dies quietly.

Have these ready before you tell anyone you exist:

- Every current item, with [a price you have worked out](/blog/how-to-price-handmade-products), on a page you can link to.
- A name, a city, and a real way to contact you.
- [Photographs that show the actual item](/blog/product-photography-with-a-phone), not a mood.
- Some indication of delivery time and what happens if something is wrong.

This is a day of work. It is worth more than a month of posting, because posting sends people to whatever you have built, and if that is nothing, the posting is wasted.

## The first ten, roughly in the order they arrive

**One to three: people who know you.** Family, friends, colleagues. Take these orders seriously — real packaging, real dispatch, real follow-up. They are your rehearsal, and they will tell you what your process gets wrong while the stakes are low.

**Four to six: people who know them.** This is the referral layer and it does not happen by itself. Ask the first three, directly and without embarrassment, if they know one person who would want this. One person, not a broadcast. Most will say yes.

**Seven to ten: strangers who were already searching.** This is the layer that matters, because it is the only one that repeats without you asking anyone for a favour. Someone was looking for a gift, a replacement, a specific material, a local maker. They found you because you existed somewhere searchable at the moment they looked.

> The first six customers prove the product works. The seventh proves the business can work, because you did not have to know them.

## Places strangers actually come from, at this size

**Search.** Someone typing what they want into Google or asking an assistant for a recommendation. This is slow to start and compounds. Everything you publish that answers a real question stays findable for years.

**Local and community groups.** Resident groups, city groups, hobby groups, alumni lists. Small, specific, and full of people who buy from people. Read the rules and contribute before you sell; most groups are tolerant of a seller who is part of the group and hostile to one who arrived to advertise.

**Markets and pop-ups.** Expensive in time, unbeatable for learning. An afternoon of watching people pick up your work and put it down teaches you more about pricing and packaging than six months of online guessing. Take a way for people to find you later — a card with a link, not just a handle.

**Other sellers.** The maker with a similar audience and a non-competing product is the most underused channel in small commerce. Not an influencer. A peer.

**Reels and short video.** Genuinely the fastest way to reach people who have never heard of you, and genuinely unreliable. Treat any single viral moment as luck you cannot repeat, and make sure it points somewhere permanent.

## The thing to do after every single order

Ask for a photograph and a line of feedback. Not a review — a line.

You want this for two reasons. The obvious one is that the next buyer will read it. The less obvious one is that the first ten orders are the only time you will get honest, unhurried feedback about the packaging, the delivery time, and whether the colour matched the photograph. Later, people just stop buying.

### A message that works

*"Thank you — it went out this morning, arriving around Thursday. When it lands, would you send me a photo of it in your home? I use them to show people what these actually look like, and I'd be grateful."*

You will get a photograph from roughly half. That is five pieces of proof from ten orders, which is the difference between a shop that looks new and a shop that looks real.

## What not to do in the first month

1. **Do not run ads.** You do not yet know which item sells, at what price, to whom. Paid traffic sent at an untested page is a fast way to buy an expensive answer to a question you could have got for free.
2. **Do not chase followers.** Four hundred people who can find your prices are worth more than ten thousand who cannot.
3. **Do not add more products.** The instinct when sales are slow is to make something new. It is almost always the presentation, the price, or the findability — not the range. Rewrite [the descriptions](/blog/how-to-write-a-product-description) before you make anything else.
4. **Do not discount to start.** A launch discount trains your first buyers to wait for the next one, and they will.

## What ten customers actually buys you

Not much revenue. What it buys you is a working process, a set of photographs of real orders in real homes, a handful of reviews, and evidence about which item people actually reach for.

That evidence is the entire input to month two. Sellers who skip the first ten and go straight to reach end up with traffic and no idea what to do with it.

Get to ten. Then look at what the ten told you.
